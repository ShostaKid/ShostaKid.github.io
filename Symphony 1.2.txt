<p align="center"><em>Allegretto ironico</em></p>

<p align="center">***</p>

<p>Afterwards, when Anna tried to find the morning it began, she could not, and this was the cruelest part, because a thing that begins on no particular morning cannot be traced back and cannot be undone, and so she settled, in the private accounting she kept of that winter, on the last morning she could prove they had still been all right.</p>

<p>It was a Thursday. She could prove it because of the orange.</p>

<p>Sasha had brought an orange to morning session, one orange, and had peeled it at the boards with her gloves off in the cold, complaining the entire time, and had split it down the middle without being asked and pressed the larger half into Anna's palm with the smaller half already in her own mouth, and had said, through the orange, that Kamila got none because Kamila had beaten her at cards on the bus and gloating was a punishable offense. Kamila had protested from the bench. Sasha had held firm. Anna had eaten the larger half segment by segment while Sasha argued jurisprudence with a fourteen-year-old, and the segments had been cold and bright and had tasted the way that whole autumn had tasted, and Anna had laughed at something Sasha said, laughed without covering her mouth, which she only did at Sambo, which she only did with them.</p>

<p>That was the last morning she could prove.</p>

<p>After that the winter came in, and the cameras came with it.</p>

<p>They arrived the way weather arrives, without a specific announcement. By December the boards on the north side had a rotation of them, coats open, notebooks on the railing, paper cups of coffee going cold in gloves, and behind every camera there was a body attached to a network attached to a country whose Olympic selection was six weeks out, and the bodies watched the group train the way people watch a river before a flood: not with malice, with anticipation, which is worse, because malice can be argued with.</p>

<p>Anna had asked Eteri, once, in the first days, whether they were allowed. Eteri had answered without looking up from her stopwatch. "They pay their money," Eteri had said, and Anna had understood that permission had never been the question, that the cameras were the same order of fact as the ice being cold, as the winter continuing to be winter.</p>

<p>What she had not understood, in the first days, was what the cameras would be pointed at. She had assumed, with the innocence she was later ashamed of, that they would be pointed at skating.</p>

<p>They were pointed at the space between her and Sasha.</p>

<p>She learned this slowly, in pieces, the way one learns the shape of a room in the dark. A physio's radio, left on during treatment, carried a sports program in which two commentators spent eleven minutes on the group and nine of those minutes on a question neither girl had ever been asked directly: whether the friendship could survive the selection. Anna, face-down on the table with her hip being worked on, listened to two grown men discuss her friendship in the past tense, hypothetically, professionally, the way meteorologists discuss a storm system, and the physio, embarrassed, had reached over and turned it off, and the silence after it was worse, because the silence confirmed that the physio had also been listening, and had also understood who the storm system was.</p>

<p>A week later it was a podcast. Sasha played it in the changing room, out loud, because Sasha's response to everything that winter was volume. A former skater with a good voice was saying, in the reasonable tone that broadcast training installs in people, that the two represented opposite philosophies of the sport, that Trusova was the future's body and Shcherbakova the past's soul, and that Beijing would decide between them, not between the girls, he was careful to say, between the philosophies, as if the girls were merely the buildings the philosophies lived in and the demolition of one building was nothing personal.</p>

<p>"The past's soul," Sasha repeated, and laughed her loudest laugh, the one with the edge under it. "You hear this? You're a museum, Anichka. I'm a bulldozer."</p>

<p>"Turn it off."</p>

<p>"It's funny."</p>

<p>"It's not funny. Turn it off."</p>

<p>Sasha turned it off. But she had laughed, and Anna had not, and that small difference in what they could each afford to find funny sat between them on the changing-room bench, a third body, patient, in no hurry, and neither of them looked at it, and it did not leave.</p>

<p>And under the cameras and under the radio and under the podcast there was the other thing, the thing with no antenna and no press badge, the thing that lived in their phones.</p>

<p>Anna did not go looking for it. It arrived on its own, the way water finds a level. A cousin sent a screenshot meaning well. A girl from the junior group left a tab open on the shared computer in the lounge. The threads had titles like a science, ANALYSIS: Tech content vs PCS ceiling, Beijing selection scenarios, and inside them thousands of strangers arranged her and Sasha into an argument, quote by quote, protocol by protocol, and the strangers were not cruel, mostly, that was the thing Anna could not explain to her mother, they were not cruel. They were interested. They wrote things like:</p>

<p><em>honestly it's fascinating that shcherbakova went full artistry this season while her best friend is out here doing five quads. like they looked at each other and chose opposite weapons</em></p>

<p>And below it, from a stranger with a sunflower avatar:</p>

<p><em>"best friend" is doing a lot of work in that sentence lol. no way that's surviving beijing</em></p>

<p>And below that, from someone whose entire username was a combination of both their names run together, a stranger who had taken their two names and made of them a single word, as if the two of them were one creature that the internet had discovered and named the way explorers name a mountain:</p>

<p><em>you don't understand. they grew up together. whatever happens in beijing one of them is going to have to hug the other one at the boards afterwards and I am NOT ready</em></p>

<p>Anna read the thread standing in the lounge with her tea going cold, and the strangers were not wrong about anything factual, that was the second thing she could not explain to her mother. The strangers had watched every protocol. The strangers knew her layout better than some of her coaches. The strangers had simply taken her life, and Sasha's life, and the orange split at the boards, and the balcony in Karelia, and nine years of mornings, and compressed all of it into a bracket: two names, one line between them, advancing toward a final neither girl had scheduled.</p>

<p>She put the phone down.</p>

<p>She did not tell Sasha about the thread.</p>

<p>She learned later, much later, that Sasha had read the same thread the same week, and had not told her either, and that the two of them had thereby begun, without one word exchanged, the specific silence that was to be the whole architecture of that winter: each of them protecting the other from a thing both of them had already seen.</p>

<p><em>Sardonico.</em></p>

<p>The first time the axis spoke out loud in the arena itself, it wore a gray coat and held a paper cup.</p>

<p>"Shcherbakova is the more artistic," the man at the boards said to the man beside him, in the tone of a person completing a checklist. "Trusova has the quads."</p>

<p>He said it without malice, on a Tuesday, at seven in the morning, and he did not know that he was not saying anything, that he was only repeating out loud, inside the building for the first time, the sentence the whole winter had already agreed on, the sentence the podcast had said in philosophy and the threads had said in brackets and the commentators had said in eleven minutes on a physio's radio. The sentence did not need him. The sentence had already been everywhere. He was only the first person careless enough to say it within earshot of the girl it was measuring.</p>

<p>Anna did not stop when she heard it. Anna's stopping had always been an event in the way stones sinking are events, in that it never happened when anyone was watching. She skated on. She reached the far boards. She turned, and began the entry to her Ruska section, and midway through the entry her arm did something her mind had not authorized, which was to reach further, to open wider, to hold the line an entire quarter-beat longer than she had held it the day before. It was a small change. It was a change made by a girl who had just been measured out loud and had found, without consulting herself, that she would rather be the thing the sentence said she was than be nothing the sentence could say at all.</p>

<p>Sasha, on the far side of the rink lacing her boot, could not have heard the sentence and could not have seen the arm, and felt both, the way one feels a temperature drop through a wall. She finished lacing. She stood. She skated to center ice without looking at anyone, planted her edge, and threw herself into a quadruple lutz, and landed it, and did not celebrate, and two turns later threw a quadruple flip, and landed it, and did not celebrate, and then skated to Eteri and said, low, in the voice she used when the paperwork only needed signing:</p>

<p>"I want a fifth quad in the Free."</p>

<p>"You have four."</p>

<p>"Five."</p>

<p>Eteri clicked her stopwatch and did not answer, and across the rink Anna's spiral carried a line it had never carried in October, and at the boards the cameras clicked in a small rolling volley, photographing, with professional satisfaction, exactly what they had come to Sambo to photograph: the two girls, sixty meters apart, each becoming the answer to the other, in an argument neither of them had started and neither of them knew how to leave.</p>

<p>That evening they met in the changing-room doorway, Anna leaving, Sasha returning. The doorway was wide enough that they did not have to acknowledge each other in order to pass. Three months ago the doorway would not have mattered; three months ago one of them would have caught the other's sleeve out of pure reflex, some fragment of the day needing to be handed over before it went stale. Now Anna's shoulder brushed Sasha's shoulder, wool against wool, and both of them said nothing, and both of them kept walking, and the nothing they said was not yet hatred and was not yet even distance, it was only tiredness, the specific tiredness of two people who knew that any sentence begun in that doorway would have to include the winter, and neither of them, that evening, had the strength to hold the winter and a sentence at the same time.</p>

<p>Behind them Kamila stopped in the doorway with her stretch band over her shoulder, and watched both of their backs going in opposite directions, and began, very quietly, to hum. The phrase climbed, five notes, toward the sixth. Kamila's mouth made the shape of the sixth note, and the sound that came out was a half-step under it, flat, wrong, and she heard the wrongness in her own throat and stopped, and swallowed, and tried again, and it came out wrong again, and she closed her mouth and stood holding her stretch band in a doorway that still smelled faintly of both of them, and did not try a third time.</p>

<p align="center">***</p>

<p><em>Meno mosso.</em></p>

<p>The stretching room at Sambo-70 was smaller than most people expected. Three mats along one wall, a mirror along the other, and a heater in the corner that had, for as long as Kamila had been in this room, ticked at irregular intervals, so that Kamila had learned to recognize the room by its tick the way older girls recognized rooms by the angle of their light. It was a small skill. It was, Kamila had recently begun to understand, the kind of skill only younger girls needed.</p>

<p>She sat on the middle mat with a foam roller under her calf, and Anna was on her right and Sasha was on her left, and this was, by December, the only room in the building where the three of them still shared the same three feet of air.</p>

<p>"Anichka," Kamila said.</p>

<p>Anna looked up. Anna's looking up had become, over the last month, a slower event than it used to be, with a pause in it, as if Anna first checked that answering to her name was the correct next move.</p>

<p>"I liked the arm you added. In the Ruska part. The one at the end."</p>

<p>"Thank you, Kamilochka."</p>

<p>"It looked like you'd been saving it."</p>

<p>Anna's answer was a smile, small and managed, and then she rolled her hip and did not say more, and on the other mat Sasha did not look up, and Kamila, watching Anna not-answering and Sasha not-listening across the length of one slow breath, understood that the first attempt had failed because Sasha had not been invited into it, and that a conversation with Anna alone, this winter, was a thing Sasha's shoulders would read as a transaction conducted at her expense.</p>

<p>She tried the other door.</p>

<p>"Sashka. They said in the press room you're adding a fifth quad."</p>

<p>Sasha looked up, fast, direct, the look of a person who had never been given a reason not to answer her own name. "Where'd you hear that."</p>

<p>"Fedosov."</p>

<p>"Fedosov gets things wrong."</p>

<p>"He got this right."</p>

<p>Sasha grinned, and the grin, in the low tick of the heater, for an audience that was only Kamila and Anna and a mirror, was almost the grin Kamila had grown up under. Almost. There was a new thing in it, a thing Kamila had no word for but knew the shape of: the shape of a grin produced because a grin had been requested.</p>

<p>"Fine. Yes. Salchow. Fifth."</p>

<p>"You'll land it."</p>

<p>"I will."</p>

<p>Kamila waited. She was good at waiting; she had spent a month discovering that waiting was the only intervention she was small enough to attempt. She waited for one of them to say, casually, meaning nothing, the other one's name. In October they had not been able to complete three sentences without it. Anichka says. Ask Sashka. That was how their sentences had been built, each girl load-bearing in the other's grammar. Kamila had never noticed it until it stopped.</p>

<p>Neither of them said it.</p>

<p>Kamila looked at the mirror. In the mirror the three of them sat in a row, and the two on the outside were not looking at each other, and the one in the middle was, without meaning to be, the only thing holding the row together, and Kamila saw this with the clarity of a girl who has watched her whole life from the middle seat, and who understands, at the exact moment the mirror shows her the row, what the middle seat is for.</p>

<p>In the summer, at the camp on the lake, the three of them had shared a room. Kamila had woken once at three in the morning to find the other two beds empty and the balcony door ajar, and through it Anna and Sasha sitting on the balcony in T-shirts, because the summer nights in Karelia held no cold worth mentioning, and Sasha had been laughing at something Anna said, laughing the laugh Kamila had grown up hearing, and Anna had been laughing with her, unguarded, uncovered, and the balcony was small enough that their shoulders touched, and Kamila had watched from the dark of the doorway without stepping into the light, because she had understood, even at fourteen, that the room she was watching was not the room she was in.</p>

<p>She had gone back to bed. She had not understood, that night, that the room she was watching was a room with an ending.</p>

<p>She understood it now, in December, on the middle mat.</p>

<p>So she did the only thing she had.</p>

<p>She hummed, out loud, deliberately, louder than she had ever hummed in front of them: the phrase, five notes climbing toward the sixth, and at the pause where the sixth should have been she held the silence open and turned her head, first one way, then the other.</p>

<p>"Does this sound right to you," Kamila said.</p>

<p>Anna stopped rolling her hip.</p>

<p>Sasha stopped rolling her ankle.</p>

<p>For a beat, for exactly eight beats, both of them looked at her. Not at each other. At her, the girl in the middle, with the phrase hanging unresolved in the ticking room and the mirror doubling all three of them into six, and Kamila, who had spent her whole small life underneath their two voices, had both of them, for eight bars, listening to hers, and she looked at Anna, and she looked at Sasha, and she opened her mouth to finish the phrase.</p>

<p>A phone dinged.</p>

<p>Sasha's, on the floor by the mat. The screen said Rostelecom and Kazan and a number and a date, and Sasha, who had been about to speak, picked it up and read it, and by the time she had read it the eight bars were over, and the room's attention had dissolved back into the winter, and Kamila was the girl in the middle again, holding a phrase no one was waiting to hear the end of.</p>

<p>She closed her mouth.</p>

<p>"Kazan," Sasha said, to no one. "They gave us Kazan."</p>

<p>Anna resumed rolling her hip.</p>

<p>Kamila looked at the mirror once more. The row was still there. The two outside girls were still not looking at each other. The girl in the middle was not humming now, because the girl in the middle had understood, for the first time, that the correct sixth note, if it existed at all, was not a note she was going to be allowed to sing.</p>

<p>Anna stood first, rolled her mat with her deliberate quiet, said good evening in three words with three words' warmth, and left. Sasha stood second, noisier, packing her mat like a person still in an argument with her own phone, and on her way out ruffled the top of Kamila's head with the absent affection of a seventeen-year-old who had stopped remembering that fourteen was still small, and left.</p>

<p>Kamila sat on her mat.</p>

<p>The heater ticked.</p>

<p>She rolled her calf twice more, and rolled up the mat, and stood, and left, and did not hum, and the room, without her, went on ticking to nobody.</p>

<p align="center">***</p>

<p><em>Come prima.</em></p>

<p>Sasha finished her own Free run-through at ten past four, and by quarter past she was at the boards with a towel around her neck and her heart still doing the after-run-through thing where it insists, for whole minutes, that the program is not over. The cameras on the north side turned toward the ice, all of them, at once, in the smooth collective pivot of a flock changing direction, because Eteri had called Anna's name.</p>

<p>Anna was already there.</p>

<p>She had warmed up on the near ice while Sasha ran her Free, and now she would run hers while Sasha watched. It was not a schedule Eteri had written. It was a schedule that had assembled itself some week neither of them could identify, out of the winter's own logic: each of them now watched the other the way you watch a fire in your own building, unable to leave, unable to help.</p>

<p>The music started.</p>

<p>The opening of Anna's program was the quiet one, the Ruska, the strings laid out slow and grave, and Anna moved into it with the first arm, and Sasha, at the boards, watched the girl and the music meet.</p>

<p>She had watched this program more times than she had watched anything in her life. She had watched it in October when it was still tender, when Anna ran it half-out and laughed at her own falls. She had watched it in November begin to sharpen. She was watching it now become the thing it had been trying all season to become, a program a fifteen-year-old could not have skated and a woman could not skate either, because it required a hinge between a childhood not quite left and an adulthood not yet granted, and Anna, at seventeen, in December, was skating on the exact last weeks of that hinge, and something in the way she committed to it said that she knew.</p>

<p>The Ruska unfolded. The slow arm. The long spiral. The arm Kamila had liked, the one at the end of the section, saved and saved and now spent, reaching further than any camera deserved.</p>

<p>And watching it, Sasha found the morning with the orange.</p>

<p>She had not thought about the orange in weeks. It surfaced now, unasked, the way the body surfaces things at the worst hour: the boards, the cold segments, gloves off, Kamila protesting jurisdiction from the bench, Anna laughing without covering her mouth. Sasha, standing at the boards in December watching the same girl skate sixty meters away, could not locate the road between that morning and this one. There had been no fight. That was what she could not explain to her mother, to Lena, to herself at two in the morning: there had been no fight. There had been a winter, and cameras, and a sentence in every language, and threads with both their names run together into one word, and somewhere inside all of that weather the two of them had let go of something without either of them opening a hand.</p>

<p>On the ice the Ruska was ending.</p>

<p>Sasha knew the score of this program the way she knew the score of her own. She knew what stood between the Ruska and the Waltz: nothing. No pause, no breath, a single hinge of choreography where the slow strings die and the Waltz comes up underneath them already turning, and the program pivots from elegy into irony in the space of two counts, and Daniil had built one gesture into that hinge, one gesture only, and Sasha, at the boards, watched it come the way you watch a wave come: the last slow arm of the Ruska descended, the strings let go, and in the two counts of silence before the Waltz seized the arena, Anna lifted her chin and smiled.</p>

<p><em>Stringendo.</em></p>

<p>It was the smile the choreography asked for. Sasha knew this. She had been in the room in August when Daniil built it; she had heard him say, here you stop mourning and start dancing, they need to see the mask go on. It was a mask; it was designed as a mask; it was, by any professional measure, a beautifully worn mask, arriving on the exact half-beat, held at the exact angle, and the Waltz came up under it and began to turn, and Anna turned with it, ironic in the shoulders, cynical in the step sequence, brilliant, and the cameras at the boards rolled in a long continuous purr, drinking it, and Sasha stood with her hands on the railing and understood the thing she had been refusing all winter to understand.</p>

<p>The mask was not going on.</p>

<p>The mask was coming off.</p>

<p>The smile at the hinge was not Anna disappearing into a character. It was the character arriving at the surface of a girl who had been performing, all winter, in the other direction: performing warmth she could no longer carry, performing a friendship the cameras had already written the obituary for, performing, in doorways, at benches, in three-word good evenings, the ordinary daily play of a person who was fine. That was the performance. The Waltz was the truth. Somewhere in the last three weeks, without a fight, without a hand opening, the girl who had held Sasha's hand on the boards in the dark had gone away and locked the door behind her, and had left, standing guard at the surface, this: a smile that arrived on the half-beat, held at the correct angle, and asked for nothing, and needed no one, and could be photographed from any side without cost, because there was no longer anything behind it that a photograph could take.</p>

<p>The Waltz turned. The smile stayed. Anna danced inside it, sixty meters and a whole winter away.</p>

<p>Sasha watched, and could not stop watching, and had never once in nine years been able to stop watching, and at the boards her knuckles went white on the railing before she noticed they were hers.</p>

<p><em>Come rintocchi.</em></p>

<p>The Waltz went on turning. The smile did not come off. Sasha understood, at last, what it had cost, and who had paid it, and that the audience it had been rehearsed for, all winter, in every doorway, was her.</p>
