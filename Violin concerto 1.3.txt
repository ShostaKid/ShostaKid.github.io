<p align="center"><em>Andante, sempre lo stesso basso; attacca il seguente</em></p>
<p align="center">***</p>

<p>The suitcase stood in the hall for eleven days without being opened, and nobody in the apartment mentioned it, and the girl it belonged to walked past it four times a day on her way to the door and did not look at it once.</p>

<p>Her skates were inside it. That was the arrangement she had come to with the hall: the suitcase held the skates, and the skates held everything else, and as long as the zip stayed closed the whole of it could stay in there together, packed flat, taking up no more room than a suitcase takes.</p>

<p>She had said the thing out loud. That was the trouble with saying things out loud; a sentence spoken into a microphone stops belonging to you within the hour, and hers had gone around the world twice before she was off the ice, and now it was a headline in six languages and a fact about her, and there was nothing to be done except be a person who had meant it. She had meant it. She still meant it. That was the part her mother could not seem to hear, however carefully she asked, in that new careful voice everyone had started using, the voice you use around a person who has recently been injured somewhere the bandage does not show.</p>

<p>She slept eleven hours a night and woke up tired. Her face in the bathroom mirror was a face she recognized without any particular interest. And the crying had stopped, which everyone in the apartment treated as an improvement, though it had not stopped so much as finished, the way rain finishes, leaving the marks of itself dried into everything: two faint tracks on either side of her nose that no amount of washing seemed to take off, that were probably not there at all, that she could see perfectly well.</p>

<p>The only fixed point in any of it was the park, and the only reason for the park was the dog.</p>

<p>Tina did not care about any of it. That was the entire basis of the relationship. Tina was a small chestnut-and-white Basenji with an outrageous curl of tail and the personality of a minor aristocrat, and she had no opinion whatsoever about scores or judges or the ordering of names, and she required, every single afternoon, without negotiation, to be taken to the park at the end of the street and walked around the pond twice.</p>

<p>So Moscow got its thaw, and its light came back an extra ten minutes a day, and the park filled up with the whole apparatus of a city remembering spring, prams and old men and children in coats too warm for the weather, the ice on the pond gone gray and rotten at the edges with the water showing black underneath, and through the middle of all that returning life a girl in a hood walked a small dog twice around a pond, at the same hour, every day, wearing the expression of somebody carrying out a sentence.</p>

<p>On the fourth afternoon, on the bench at the far side of the pond, there was a girl with chestnut hair, reading a book.</p>

<p align="center">***</p>

<p>She did not look up.</p>

<p>That was the first thing, and the strangest, and the thing Sasha turned over for the rest of that evening. Anna Shcherbakova was sitting on a public bench in a public park two streets from Sasha's apartment with her coat buttoned and her gloves on and a paperback open in her hands, and when the taller girl came around the pond with a Basenji on a short lead she did not lift her head, did not smile, did not perform any of the small violence of recognition. She turned a page.</p>

<p>Sasha walked past her at a distance of six meters with the lead wrapped once around her fist and her hand a palm's width from the collar, and did not slow down, and did not speak, and did not look, and went home.</p>

<p>The next day the bench was occupied at the same hour.</p>

<p>The day after that as well.</p>

<p>By the fifth day it had stopped being a coincidence and had not yet become anything else, and Sasha lay on her bed that evening in her coat with the light going out of the window and tried to find the handle on the thing, and could not.</p>

<p>Anger would have been usable. She had been running on anger since February and it worked, it got her from room to room, it made her difficult and it made her fast. Even hatred would have given her something to work with. But that bench had nothing in it to push off, and it took her three afternoons to place the feeling, and when she placed it she turned her face into the pillow, because she had known it for years and had complained about it for years and had never once been able to say what was wrong with it.</p>

<p>It was the slow music. It was every piece Anna had ever chosen and every piece Sasha had ever called boring, the kind with no beat in it hard enough to hit, nothing coming at you that could be answered, nothing anywhere to jump to. You could not fight it. You could not get above it. It simply went on underneath at its own speed while you did whatever you were going to do, and it did not need you to do anything at all.</p>

<p>She walked the pond twice the next day and kept the lead short. She did not once consider going the other way around. She noticed that she had not considered it, on about the eighth day, and put the noticing somewhere she did not have to look at it.</p>

<p align="center">***</p>

<p>It was the dog who broke the arrangement, because of course it was.</p>

<p>Basenjis do not bark. It is the first thing anyone tells you about the breed and the least useful, because what they do instead is exactly what they like, at whatever moment they like, with the serene indifference of an animal that has never once accepted the theory of ownership. Tina had been walking that pond for eleven days past a bench, and on the twelfth she simply stopped, and put her nose up into the wind coming off the water, and turned toward the bench, and went.</p>

<p>The lead ran out of Sasha's fist to its full extension before she had finished understanding what was happening.</p>

<p>Tina crossed the six meters without any hesitation at all. Not the wary approach of a dog toward a stranger, none of the ritual of introduction; she went the way a dog goes toward a coat she has slept on, toward hands she has been fed from at a kitchen table, toward the specific smell of a person who has been in the apartment enough times that the apartment has learned it, and she put her front paws up on the bench and pushed her head under the paperback.</p>

<p>Anna set the book down on her knee. She scratched the dog behind one ear, then under the chin, then along the jaw, exactly the way you do it if you know that this particular dog will tolerate the first two and only actually wants the third.</p>

<p>She did not look up.</p>

<p>And Sasha stood six meters away at the end of a lead she could have shortened at any time, with a Basenji leaning her entire ridiculous weight against the shins of the last person on earth she wanted to see, and did not shorten it. She told herself it was because dragging a dog off a bench by the throat in a public park was not a thing she was going to be photographed doing. She told herself several things. She stood there for four minutes and thirty seconds by the clock on the far pavilion, and when Tina finally came back of her own accord, Sasha turned and walked home with the lead at half length and her jaw set so hard her teeth ached.</p>

<p>The bench was occupied the next day. And the next.</p>

<p align="center">***</p>

<p>She broke on a Thursday, in the rain, because Tina had gone to the bench again and this time had lain down under it with the total commitment of a dog who has selected her afternoon.</p>

<p>"Why do you keep coming here?"</p>

<p>It came out of Sasha louder than the park required. Two prams changed course. Anna closed the book over one finger and looked up, and her face did the thing it always did, which was almost nothing, and it was the almost-nothing that finished it.</p>

<p>"You can stop," Sasha said. "Whatever this is. Whatever you think you're doing. You can stop."</p>

<p>Nothing. Rain on the pond, one gray coin at a time.</p>

<p>"You want to know what's funny? All those years." Her voice had found the register she had used on every microphone since February, and it was easier there, and it was worse there, and she went on anyway. "The buses. The screws. All of it. You've built something in your head out of that, I can see it from here, you've built a whole thing. But we trained at the same rink. That's it. That's the entire fact. We were two girls with the same schedule and a hundred and fifty hours a week in the same building, and you can call that whatever you like, but I'm telling you what it was, and it was a schedule." The lead had gone tight in her fist. "You got what you came for. So go and be happy about it somewhere I don't have to look at you."</p>

<p>The rain went on doing what it had been doing.</p>

<p>Anna's face did not change. Her eyes did not fill. She sat on a soaked bench in a public park with a dog asleep across her boots, and looked up at the girl half a head taller than her for perhaps three seconds.</p>

<p>Then she looked down, and closed the book. Not over her finger. All the way.</p>

<p>Sasha got the dog. The dog did not want to come and came. She was out of the park in ninety seconds and up the stairs of the building in forty more, and she did not cry, because that was finished, and she sat on the floor of the hall with her back against a suitcase full of skates and her wet coat still on and shook for a while, from the cold, obviously, from the rain.</p>

<p><em>Tacet.</em></p>

<p>She did not go to the park the next day. Or the day after. Or the day after that.</p>

<p>She walked the dog around the block instead, twice a day, badly, on wet pavement between parked cars, and Tina made her opinion of this arrangement known through the medium of sitting down in doorways.</p>

<p align="center">***</p>

<p>On the fourth day the weather turned bright and the dog won.</p>

<p>The park was full. The ice on the pond had gone entirely, and the black water was throwing back a sky that had not been available in Moscow since October, and the whole place was loud with the specific noise of a city that has survived another winter and would like to say so.</p>

<p>The bench at the far side had an old man on it, with a fat elderly spaniel, and no one else.</p>

<p>Sasha walked her lap. On the far turn she looked back at the bench, which was a thing her head did without asking her, and the bench was still an old man and a spaniel, and she walked the second lap and looked again, and then, because Tina had stopped to investigate something and there was no reason to go home yet, she walked a third, which she had never done, and by the time she came round again she had checked her phone twice for messages that had not arrived and were not going to.</p>

<p>The old man watched all of this with the frank interest of a man who has been coming to this park for forty years and considers it his to observe.</p>

<p>"Four," he said.</p>

<p>Sasha stopped. "Sorry?"</p>

<p>"Laps. You've done four." He nodded at Tina, who had sat down on the path in protest. "Look at your dog. She's finished. She's been finished since the second one. You're walking a dog that isn't being walked anymore, young lady, you're just going round."</p>

<p>"I like walking."</p>

<p>"Mm," said the old man, in a tone that would have done credit to a certain bench occupant, and scratched his spaniel's ear. Then, to the spaniel, comfortably: "She's not here today."</p>

<p>"I don't know who you mean."</p>

<p>"No," he agreed. "Small one. Brown hair. Book." He counted them off on three fingers as though establishing the specification of a machine part. "Sits there, second bench, every afternoon, an hour. Never eats. Never talks to anybody. Reads the same book for a week and a half, which tells you something, because either it's a very good book or she isn't turning the pages."</p>

<p>Sasha stood on the path and said nothing at all.</p>

<p>"She was here Thursday," he added. "In that rain. The whole hour. I said to her, girl, go home, you'll catch something, and she said thank you very much, she'd stay a bit." He shook his head slowly at the vast general foolishness of the modern world. "In my day we went indoors when it rained. Of course, in my day we also queued four hours for cheese, so I don't say we had it all worked out either."</p>

<p>Something turned over in Sasha's chest.</p>

<p>Her face did absolutely nothing, which is a skill, which is the first skill a girl like her learns and the last one she loses. "Mm," she said.</p>

<p>"Mm," the old man agreed, satisfied, and went back to his spaniel, and did not mention it again, and had, in the manner of his kind, said everything he came to say and gone back to his own business without ever once appearing to know it.</p>

<p>She walked home the long way, and did not remember any of it.</p>

<p align="center">***</p>

<p>The bench was occupied the following afternoon.</p>

<p>Anna did not look up. Sasha walked past with the lead at full length hanging from her fist, and made it eleven meters past the bench before she slowed, and did not turn around, and stopped anyway, and stood facing the pond, watching a duck do nothing of interest whatsoever for a very long time.</p>

<p>Behind her, quite quietly, in the flat unhurried way of somebody who has been doing it for a while, Anna was reading aloud.</p>

<p>Not to her. Sasha understood that at once and completely; there was no performance in it, no volume aimed anywhere, no invitation. She was reading to the dog, who had gone over again, and who was lying across her boots with her chin on the toe of one of them, and the sentences came off that bench at a pitch designed to reach one animal and no further.</p>

<p>Sasha stood eleven meters away with her back turned and listened to a girl read four hundred pages about a man who could not decide whether to visit his mother, and did not move, and did not go, and stayed until the light went.</p>

<p>The lead hung slack from her fingers the whole time, and at some point, without her noticing, the loop of it slipped off her wrist and lay on the path, and the dog did not run, and nobody picked it up.</p>

<p align="center">***</p>

<p>She went back on a Tuesday, before six in the morning, without telling anybody, with her hood up and the suitcase finally open in the hall behind her.</p>

<p>The building smelled the way it had always smelled. The resurfacer came off at 5:52. The lights over the far end came up at 5:58, and Alexandra Trusova stepped onto ice for the first time in a season and stood there in the cold clean silence of an empty rink at six in the morning with her legs shaking, and then she skated, badly, slowly, with none of the jumps and none of the plans and none of the noise, one lap and then another, the way you walk around a room you used to live in.</p>

<p>She was three-quarters of an hour into it when the door at the tunnel end opened.</p>

<p>Anna stopped at the boards with her guards in her hand and her bag still on her shoulder, and looked out at the ice, and it was the first time in eleven years that anybody at Khrustalny had arrived at that hour to find the ice already occupied.</p>

<p>She did not say anything about that. She did not say anything about the season, or the suitcase, or the park, or the rain, or the Thursday. She put her guards on the boards, and stepped down, and skated her own patterns at her own end for twenty minutes, at the exact speed she had skated them at eleven, at fifteen, at every unbroken morning in between, and when the hour was over she came across the ice on her way to the gate and stopped in front of the taller girl.</p>

<p>She reached into her jacket pocket and held something out.</p>

<p>A black hair elastic. Ordinary. The kind that lives in every skater's pocket in the world.</p>

<p>"Your left side's coming loose," Anna said.</p>

<p>Then she went to the gate, and put her guards on, and left, because the thing that never varies does not stop to see whether it has been noticed.</p>

<p>The tunnel door swung shut behind her, and the sound of it went out across the empty ice and came back off the far boards, and went out again, thinner, and came back thinner still, and after a while there was only the building's own low hum in the roof, and one girl standing in the middle of a sheet of ice with her fist closed around a hair elastic, and nothing anywhere left to fight.</p>
