<p align="center"><em>III. Allegro vivace; e la viola, finalmente, canta</em></p>
<p align="center">***</p>

<p>On the thirty-seventh day Kamila Valieva put on outdoor clothes for the first time since February and discovered that her coat was too big for her at the shoulders, which was new, and that she had forgotten which pocket she kept her keys in, which was worse.</p>

<p>She stood in the hall for a while. Her mother, in the kitchen doorway with a dish towel and an expression of enormous and carefully constructed indifference, said nothing at all about it, and Kamila loved her fiercely for that and could not have said so, and went down four flights of stairs on legs that had been designed for entirely different work and had been sitting in a gray room for five weeks disagreeing with the design.</p>

<p>The street door was heavier than she remembered.</p>

<p>The light outside came at her from every direction at once, off windows, off the wet road, off the roof of a parked car, and she stood on the top step with her eyes streaming and one hand up like somebody arriving from a cave, and when the world resolved itself there was a girl in a dark coat leaning against the railing at the bottom with her ankles crossed and her hands in her pockets, entirely unhurried, as though she had been standing there since Thursday.</p>

<p>"Eleven minutes late," Anna said. "I had money on quarter past."</p>

<p>Kamila came down two steps and stopped. Her throat had done something complicated and she did not trust it yet.</p>

<p>"How did you know I would even come down?"</p>

<p>"Oh, please." The girl with the chestnut hair pushed off the railing and came up the steps to meet her, and took hold of the front of her coat, and pulled the collar straight with two sharp practical tugs, the way she had been straightening this particular collar since it belonged to a child. "You waited fifty minutes outside a physiotherapy room once to ask me about a twizzle. Fifty. I timed it, I was hiding. My little fourteen-year-old Valieva does not stay behind a door because somebody told her to."</p>

<p>"I'm fifteen."</p>

<p>"You're fourteen forever. It was decided at the time." The hands came off the collar and closed around both of Kamila's, and Anna looked down at them with an expression that did not change at all. "You're freezing. Where are your gloves?"</p>

<p>"I don't know where anything is."</p>

<p>"Then you'll have mine, and I'll have my pockets, and we will both survive it." She was already pulling them off. "Give me your hand. No, the other one, that one's worse."</p>

<p>And Kamila laughed, and the sound came out of her wrong, cracked and far too loud for the street, and she put her hand over her mouth, and Anna took it down off her mouth without comment and held onto it while she pushed the glove on, finger by finger, like dressing somebody small.</p>

<p>"Come on," she said. "You're eating first. Everything else in the plan is after that."</p>

<p>"There's a plan?"</p>

<p>"There is always a plan, Kamilochka. You have simply never been important enough to be inside one before."</p>

<p align="center">***</p>

<p>The place was three streets away and had six tables and a window with the whole morning coming through it, and Anna ordered for both of them without opening the menu or asking a single question.</p>

<p>What arrived was blini, and sour cream, and a small bowl of honey, and tea, and Kamila looked at the plate and felt her stomach turn over with something that was not hunger and was not fear and had elements of both.</p>

<p>"I can't eat all of that."</p>

<p>"I know. Eat what you want. I'll finish it, I have no shame and no competition until October."</p>

<p>So Kamila ate. The first mouthful was nothing, texture and heat, the way food had been for five weeks. The second was warm and slightly sweet at the edge where the honey had run in. The third came with the smell of it up through her nose, butter and hot flour, and behind that, arriving whole and without any warning at all, a Sunday morning in Kazan with her grandmother's arm in a bowl and the window fogged and the radio on too loud, and Kamila put the fork down and pressed the back of her wrist hard against her mouth and sat with her eyes shut for four or five seconds.</p>

<p>Anna went on pouring tea. She poured it slowly, with her whole attention on the pot, and did not look up, and by the time she set the cup down and pushed it across, the girl opposite her had picked up the fork again.</p>

<p>"It's good," Kamila said, when her voice was available.</p>

<p>"Of course it's good, I've been doing reconnaissance for two weeks." Anna took a blini off the plate with her fingers, which was a thing Kamila had never once seen her do and did not know she did. "There was a place on Pyatnitskaya with better reviews and a woman at the counter who looked at me as though I had come to repossess something. So we're here. Eat the one with the honey, that one's the whole reason for the honey."</p>

<p>"You did reconnaissance."</p>

<p>"I did a great deal of reconnaissance," said Anna, with dignity, licking sour cream off her thumb, "and you will eat all of it and say nothing further about my methods."</p>

<p>They stayed there an hour. Kamila ate more than she had eaten at one sitting since the winter, and did not notice until she had finished, and Anna, who had ordered enough for four people precisely so that this arithmetic would be impossible to perform in real time, paid the bill while Kamila was in the bathroom and was standing at the door with both coats when she came out.</p>

<p align="center">***</p>

<p>They walked.</p>

<p>The city was in the middle of that fortnight when Moscow stops being a place people endure and becomes a place people live in: everything running, everything dripping, the pavements dark and shining and the gutters full of the whole winter going somewhere else in a hurry, and the noise of it everywhere, water and traffic and the shouting of small children released too early from too many coats.</p>

<p>Anna's plan proceeded. That was the thing Kamila noticed first and thought nothing of: the plan simply worked, item after item, the way a plan does when somebody has walked the route beforehand. They went along the embankment, where the sun came off the water hard enough to hurt. They stood on a bridge for eleven minutes doing nothing at all. They went past the bakery on Sadovaya so that Kamila could see the singing man for herself, and he was singing, badly, and the bread in the window did have burnt bottoms, and Kamila stood on the pavement outside a bakery she had been reading about for two weeks and laughed until she had to hold the railing.</p>

<p>And at half past one, at the corner by the park gates, exactly as they came round it, there was a noise like a small aircraft.</p>

<p>"KAMILOCHKA."</p>

<p>Sasha hit her at speed and lifted her clean off the pavement, which was easier than it should have been and which neither of them mentioned, and Alena arrived a half second behind with an entire bag of something warm from a stall and no explanation, and for twenty minutes the corner of that street belonged to four girls having an argument about whether Alena had, in fact, ever been able to do a proper Rippon, with demonstrations, on wet paving stones, in public, to the visible alarm of a man selling flowers.</p>

<p>Kamila laughed until her stomach hurt. Sasha kept her hand on her shoulder the entire time, not obviously, not making a thing of it, just there, the way you keep a hand on something you have recently got back.</p>

<p>And then, at ten to two, Alena looked at her phone and made a noise of horror about a fitting, and Sasha said something about a train, and they were gone up the street shouting insults at each other, and the corner was quiet, and Kamila stood on the wet pavement with a paper bag of something warm in her hands.</p>

<p>She looked at the girl beside her.</p>

<p>"They just happened to be here."</p>

<p>"Extraordinary coincidence," Anna agreed, and started walking.</p>

<p align="center">***</p>

<p>The hill was above the river on the far side of the park, and it was not much of a hill, and by the time they had walked up it Kamila's legs had made their position on the matter absolutely clear.</p>

<p>They sat on the wall at the top with the whole of the south of the city laid out under them in the flat gold light of a spring afternoon, roofs and cranes and the river going through the middle of it all with the light broken up on the water, and the wind came up the slope carrying the smell of thawed earth and diesel and, from somewhere below, a burnt-sugar smell out of a kiosk.</p>

<p>Kamila sat with her hands around her knees and looked at it and understood, with no warning and no ceremony, that all of it had been here the entire time.</p>

<p>All of it. The trams and the singing baker and the dog with an arrangement and the two women on the bench and the whole enormous indifferent machinery of a city, running for thirty-seven days behind a set of closed curtains, and it had not once slowed down for her, and it had not once turned to look, and she had been lying in a gray room with her fingers on her own throat believing that everything had stopped, and nothing had stopped. Nothing at all. It had gone on being spring, and the ice had come off the pond in a single night, and a lilac at the end of Sadovaya had come out three weeks early and disgraced itself, and none of it had asked her permission.</p>

<p>It should have been unbearable. She sat with it for a while and found that it was the opposite, that it was in fact the first thing in five weeks that had made her want to stand up, and she could not have explained why, and she did not try.</p>

<p>Beside her, Anna said nothing, and let her have it.</p>

<p>The wind came up the slope. And Kamila, turning to say something about the cranes, saw it.</p>

<p>Anna's hands were folded in her lap on top of her own gloves, left over right, and the thumb of the upper hand was moving on the leather, once, twice, small, an absolutely private motion in a public place, out of sight of anybody who was not sitting where Kamila was sitting.</p>

<p>The exact gesture. Eleven seconds of a broadcast, five times through, the fifth time with the sound off. A plastic chair in a room built for celebrating, and a girl sitting very straight with a soft toy in her lap and everybody else's catastrophe coming through the wall.</p>

<p>Kamila looked at the hands, and then at the profile above them, which was calm and pleasant and turned toward the city, and then she looked back out at the river and ran the whole day through from the beginning, and found it.</p>

<p>The plan had worked perfectly. Everything in it had worked: the food ordered before she could refuse it, the bill paid while she was out of the room, the bakery, the corner at half past one, the twenty minutes and not a minute more, this hill at this hour with the light on it. A day built by somebody with a ruler, for her, item by item, without one thing going wrong anywhere in it.</p>

<p>And not once, in five hours, had that person answered a single question about herself.</p>

<p>Kamila had asked. She had asked four times, she counted them back, and every time the answer had been a small elegant nothing and a new subject, delivered so smoothly that she had let it go, four separate times, because that was how well it was done.</p>

<p>Everything today had been right. There was exactly one thing wrong on the whole hill, and it had been sitting beside her since eleven o'clock, hiding inside a perfect plan.</p>

<p><em>Cadenza.</em></p>

<p>"Anya."</p>

<p>"Mm."</p>

<p>"You said in the letter you would teach me something today."</p>

<p>"I did." Anna's hands stopped moving. "We'll do it on ice. Not now. When you're ready, and it doesn't have to be soon."</p>

<p>"Your hands are doing the thing."</p>

<p>A pause. "What?"</p>

<p>"The thumb. On the leather." Kamila kept her eyes on the river, because if she had looked at her she would not have got any further. "You did it that night. In the room after, with the plastic chair. There's eleven seconds of it at the end of the broadcast and then they cut away, and I watched it five times, and the fifth time I turned the sound off, and that's when I saw your hands, and you were doing exactly what you're doing now."</p>

<p>The wind came up the slope and went over them and away toward the river.</p>

<p>"It was a long night," Anna said pleasantly. "Everybody was tired."</p>

<p>"Don't." It came out too loud and Kamila did not bring it down. "Don't do the." She made a small useless motion with one gloved hand, which meant the plan, and the tone, and four questions asked and got around. "That."</p>

<p>Anna said nothing.</p>

<p>"You told me at the gate, when I was fourteen. You said it isn't a stop. You said if you stop it's dead, you have to still be going underneath, the whole time." She turned her head. "Anya. You stopped."</p>

<p>"Kamila."</p>

<p>"You won." Her voice climbed and cracked in the middle of the word and she went on over the top of it. "You won, and you sat in that room by yourself with a toy on your knees and nobody came in. Nobody. And everyone watched three girls that night and they only found two of us interesting, and nobody has said it out loud, not one person, not in seven weeks, in any language, that it was."</p>

<p>She stopped, because the word was not going to come out in one piece.</p>

<p>"That it was cruel," she said, further down, quieter. "So I'm saying it."</p>

<p>Her face was wet. She had not noticed it starting and she scrubbed at it with the back of the borrowed glove, furious, the way you deal with a thing that is interrupting you.</p>

<p>"I asked you four times today," she said. "Four times, how you were. And you got out of it four times and I let you, because you're so good at it, you're so good at it that everybody just lets you, they've been letting you since February."</p>

<p>She got her breath back.</p>

<p>"I'm not going to let you."</p>

<p>Anna did not answer.</p>

<p>What happened instead was very small and Kamila, watching for it, saw all of it: the chin came down about a centimeter, and stayed there; and the composed pleasant face did not crumple, because it had been trained out of crumpling before either of them was born; and the muscle at the side of the jaw moved once; and the hands in the lap came apart and then, having nowhere to go, folded back together in a different order.</p>

<p>And her eyes filled, and did not spill, and she looked out at the city and let them dry in the wind, and did not wipe them, because wiping them would have been admitting they were there.</p>

<p>Kamila did not say anything else.</p>

<p>That was the whole of what she had learned in five weeks in a gray room, and it had been taught to her twelve times, by post: you do not ask, you do not fix, you do not say strong or pass or think of you. You put something down on the floor of the room and you leave it there and you come back on Thursday. So the youngest girl in the family sat on a wall on a hill above Moscow with her hands around her knees and said nothing at all, for a long time, at the side of somebody who had gone quiet, and simply stayed there.</p>

<p>After a while Anna's shoulder came to rest against hers. It arrived by degrees, as though it had happened by accident and could be denied later if necessary, and Kamila did not move at all, in case moving ended it.</p>

<p>They sat like that while the light came off the roofs and the cranes stopped moving in the distance somewhere.</p>

<p>Then Kamila reached into the inside pocket of a coat that was too big at the shoulders and took out four sheets of paper folded in four, with the fold gone soft from thirty-seven days of being opened and refolded in a drawer, and put them into Anna's hands.</p>

<p>"What is this?"</p>

<p>"A letter. I wrote it at two in the morning and it's four pages and the handwriting gets bad after the first one." Kamila closed Anna's fingers over it with both hands, the way you hand someone something you cannot take back. "Don't read it now. Read it when you're by yourself, because you're going to want to be, and I don't need to watch."</p>

<p>Anna looked at the folded paper in her hand for a long moment.</p>

<p>Then she put it in her inside pocket, over her chest, and buttoned the coat, and said, in a voice that was not entirely under her control and that she made no particular effort to bring back under it, "All right."</p>

<p align="center">***</p>

<p>The sun went down behind the cranes and the cold came up off the river the way it does in a Moscow spring, without any warning and with real malice, and neither of them mentioned going home.</p>

<p>Kamila put two fingers to the side of her own throat, out of habit, the way she had done ten thousand times before an entry, and found it there: quick, light, running along at a speed the team doctor had once described as within range for a bird, going about its business under her fingers as though it had never been anywhere else.</p>

<p>She took her hand down and looked out at the city and, without any decision at all, began to hum.</p>

<p>Five notes, low in the throat, climbing a little way. It was the first sound of that kind she had made in thirty-seven days and it came out rough and off-pitch and absolutely unmistakable, and she went up the way it went up, and reached the fifth note, and held it out there in the cold with the whole of the south of Moscow going about its evening underneath.</p>

<p>And then she did not stop.</p>

<p>The sixth note was nothing much. It was not a triumph and it was not an ending; it was only the phrase going on past the place it had always stopped, the way a phrase will when somebody finally lets it, and the seventh came after it and was nothing much either, and by then it had stopped being anybody's stolen five notes and had become a small ordinary tune that a girl was humming on a hill.</p>

<p>Beside her, half a beat late, in a register nobody in the world had ever heard her use, Anna came in underneath.</p>

<p align="center">***</p>

<p>The rink in October was the loudest place in Moscow.</p>

<p>The new group had six juniors in it who had all decided at once that Kamila Valieva was theirs, and they followed her around the boards asking questions with no answers, and Alena had taken to arriving early purely to referee, and the ice at four in the afternoon was full of noise and cold spray and somebody's music leaking out of somebody's speaker, and Kamila came off it at the break with her hair coming down and her face red and her lungs going.</p>

<p>She dropped onto the bench and dug through her bag for the tape she was certain she had packed, and did not find it, and found instead her phone, lit up and face down under a towel.</p>

<p>She turned it over.</p>

<p><em>What do you want to eat tonight?</em></p>

<p>Kamila sat on a cold bench in a loud building with her boots half unlaced, and looked at it for a while, and then put the phone face up on the bench beside her, where she could see it, and got on with the tape.</p>
