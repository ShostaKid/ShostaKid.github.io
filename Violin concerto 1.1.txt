<p align="center"><em>Allegretto agitato, sotto voce; e nel finale, dissolvendosi</em></p>
<p align="center">***</p>

<p>The first time Alexandra Trusova took any notice of Anna Shcherbakova, she was eleven years old, had landed nine triples that morning and knew the number the way other children know their own address, and had absolutely no intention of being interested in anybody.</p>

<p>Interest was Sasha's currency, and she spent it lavishly, at the top of her lungs, on anything that made noise. Coaches who yelled. Coaches who refused to yell. The boys' hockey team through the glass. The older girls fighting over music. Dogs, all dogs, without exception. Ice that was too hard, ice that was too soft, and the sound her own blade made on a landing when the landing was perfect, which was a sound she would have listened to for an hour if anybody had offered. What she did not spend interest on was anything that arrived quietly, because quiet things, in Sasha's experience of the world so far, rarely repaid the cost of looking twice.</p>

<p>The girl on the far side of the rink was quiet. That was the first data point, and it should have been the last.</p>

<p>She worked in the corner by the barrier where the light came in gray off the high windows, and she worked the way water works at stone, without hurry and without stopping, the same edge, the same turn, the same three meters of ice, over and over, while half the rink went past her in bright loud arcs and she did not once look up to see who was passing. Her braid was pinned. Her jacket was zipped to the throat. Sasha, gliding by on her way to somewhere louder, took in the whole of her in one pass and formed the opinion she would hold for the next year and a half: too neat, too slow, skates too clean. Nobody whose skates are that clean has ever fallen hard enough to be worth watching.</p>

<p>She landed her tenth triple twenty minutes later and forgot the girl in the corner completely, or believed that she had.</p>

<p>Eleven-year-olds are not equipped to notice the difference between forgetting a thing and putting it somewhere.</p>

<p align="center">***</p>

<p>The friendship was founded, two winters later, on a screw.</p>

<p>It was the end of an afternoon session, the corridor outside the changing rooms full of the particular exhausted cheerfulness of thirteen-year-olds released from ice, and Sasha was sitting on the floor with her boot in her lap and a blade mount that had shed a screw somewhere in the last hour and a half.</p>

<p>"It's gone," she announced to the corridor at large. "It's actually gone. I have looked. I have looked twice."</p>

<p>A shadow arrived and stopped in front of her, and a hand came down, holding a small tin.</p>

<p>"Take two," Anna said. "You'll lose one on the way home."</p>

<p>Sasha looked up. Anna Shcherbakova was standing over her with her coat already buttoned and her bag already on her shoulder, holding out a tin of blade screws sorted, Sasha would discover later, by length.</p>

<p>"Why do you have these?"</p>

<p>"Because people lose them."</p>

<p>"People. Which people?"</p>

<p>"Everyone in this building," Anna said. "Constantly."</p>

<p>Sasha took two. She tightened the mount right there on the floor with the small screwdriver Anna produced from the same bag, entirely unsurprised at her own lack of surprise, and then, because she had received something and Sasha's accounts never stayed open longer than four minutes, she stood up and announced that she was walking Anna back to the dorm.</p>

<p>"It's fifty meters."</p>

<p>"Two screws, fifty meters. That's the rate. Don't argue with the rate."</p>

<p>So they walked the fifty meters, and it took eleven minutes, because Sasha stopped twice to demonstrate things that could not be demonstrated in a coat, and by the end of it she had learned that Anna knew the name of every woman who cleaned the corridors at Khrustalny, and Anna had learned that Sasha could not walk in a straight line while talking, which was information she would find useful for years.</p>

<p>At the dorm gate, Anna said, "You do know they're supposed to be checked before every session."</p>

<p>"They are checked."</p>

<p>"By whom?"</p>

<p>"By me. Enthusiastically."</p>

<p>"Mm," Anna said, and went inside, and Sasha stood there in the cold trying to work out whether she had just been insulted, and discovered, walking back to a rink she had no reason to return to, that she was smiling about it.</p>

<p align="center">***</p>

<p>What followed was two years of the sort of friendship nobody plans and nobody notices building, made almost entirely out of buses.</p>

<p>They went everywhere by bus in those years, to test skates, to competitions in cities with unpronounceable names, four hours out and four hours back with the windows fogging and somebody's music leaking out of somebody's headphones, and on every one of those buses Anna Shcherbakova read a book and Alexandra Trusova physically could not sit still.</p>

<p>"What's it about?"</p>

<p>"You wouldn't like it."</p>

<p>"You always say that."</p>

<p>"You've never liked one yet."</p>

<p>"Because you pick books where nothing happens." Sasha hung over the back of the seat. "Go on. What happens?"</p>

<p>Anna turned a page. "A man is deciding whether to visit his mother."</p>

<p>"...And?"</p>

<p>"That's the book."</p>

<p>"That's the whole book? How long is it?"</p>

<p>"Four hundred pages."</p>

<p>"Four hundred pages of a man deciding." Sasha slumped backward into her own seat with the boneless despair of the young. "Anya. I could learn a jump in four hundred pages. I could learn two."</p>

<p>"You could learn one," Anna said, without looking up, "and lose the screws out of both boots doing it."</p>

<p>Sasha laughed loudly enough that the coach in the front row turned around, and settled, and was quiet for perhaps ninety seconds, and then said, in a completely different voice, "Read it out loud."</p>

<p>"You'll hate it."</p>

<p>"Then I'll hate it out loud and you'll have company."</p>

<p>So Anna read out loud. Quietly, in the third row of a bus outside Kazan, in a voice pitched so that only the seat in front of her could hear it, and Sasha, who had never listened to anything for four hundred pages in her life, listened for forty minutes with her cheek against the cold window and her hood up and her feet on the seat, and by the end of the trip the man had still not visited his mother, and Sasha was violently invested in whether he would.</p>

<p>After that it was a thing. It was not discussed. On the way out Anna read to herself, because Sasha was too wound up before competitions to hear words; on the way home, in the dark, with the ice already becoming a story they told each other about what had gone wrong, Anna read aloud, and Sasha put her head against the window and let the sentences come. Other girls slept. Somebody's phone played something tinny. The heater rattled. Anna's voice went on, level and unhurried, laying down one sentence at a time the way she laid down one edge at a time, until whatever was still ringing in Sasha from the day had gone quiet enough to sleep through.</p>

<p>It was also on those buses that Sasha began, without announcing it, to feed her.</p>

<p>It started with half a sandwich pushed across a seat. It became a routine and then a small war. Anna would arrive with an apple and a bottle of water and Sasha would arrive with the contents of a small canteen, and the transfer would occur.</p>

<p>"I have food."</p>

<p>"You have an apple and a philosophy."</p>

<p>"Sasha."</p>

<p>"Eat the bread. You can hate me while you eat the bread, that's allowed."</p>

<p>And Anna, who argued with coaches and judges and her own body and never once with Sasha about this, would eat the bread. Sasha did not think about why she watched to make sure. She had noticed, at some point, that Anna's wrists were smaller than they had been in the autumn, and had put the noticing somewhere, in that drawer she did not know she was renting, and had gone on stuffing bread into a girl who would not ask for it.</p>

<p>The count, meanwhile, went on. Nine triples. Eleven. Fourteen on a very good Tuesday. The number was Sasha's way of proving to the world that she existed, and she reported it, loudly, to anyone in earshot, and Anna, who was in earshot more often than anyone, received these reports with the gravity of a bank teller.</p>

<p>"Fourteen."</p>

<p>"Mm."</p>

<p>"Anya. Fourteen."</p>

<p>"I heard you the first time. And the second time, in the corridor. And the third time, to Daniil, who was on the telephone."</p>

<p>"You could be happy for me."</p>

<p>"I am extremely happy for you," Anna said, turning a page. "I'm happy for you at a volume appropriate to a bus."</p>

<p align="center">***</p>

<p>The quadruple Salchow arrived when she was thirteen, on a Thursday, and it arrived exactly once.</p>

<p>She landed it out of nothing, out of a session that had been going badly, and the rink went off like a stadium, and Eteri said one word, and Sasha spent the next twenty minutes attempting it again and falling four times in a row, because the body has opinions and one of them is that miracles are not repeatable to order. She sat on the ice after the fourth fall, laughing, with her hip already coloring, and looked around for the person she wanted to look at, which was a decision she did not remember making.</p>

<p>Anna was at the boards. She had stopped mid-lap to watch, which she never did.</p>

<p>She did not applaud. She did not shout, the way the rest of the rink had shouted. She waited until Sasha had skated over, breathing hard, grinning like a dog with a stolen shoe, and then she said, quietly, "Do it again on Monday."</p>

<p>"Monday? Anya, I just, did you see, I actually..."</p>

<p>"I saw." One eyebrow went up a millimeter and stayed there. "Anyone can do a thing once. Do it again on Monday and I'll say something nice."</p>

<p>"You are the worst person at this rink."</p>

<p>"I'm the only person at this rink who thinks you'll do it again on Monday," Anna said, and pushed off, and left Sasha standing at the boards with her heart going for reasons that had stopped being entirely about the jump.</p>

<p>She did it again on Monday. Anna said something nice. Sasha remembered the sentence for years, and never once repeated it to anybody, which was unlike her in every measurable way.</p>

<p>The count changed its denomination after that. Triples stopped being worth counting. One quad in a session became the number, then two on a strong day, then three, and the whole rink began arranging itself around the arithmetic, and by fourteen the headlines had found the arithmetic too and printed it in bold.</p>

<p align="center">***</p>

<p>She was fourteen, and it was March, and she had come down badly on her right ankle the previous evening, one quiet bad landing at the end of a long session, no drama, no wail.</p>

<p>Eteri took one look at her walk in the morning and said, in the voice reserved for absolute decrees, "You are watching today."</p>

<p>Watching. Sasha opened her mouth, and closed it again on inspection of the face across from her, and went to the boards with a book she had no intention of reading, and sat.</p>

<p>The rink emptied slowly toward the middle of the afternoon. A coach at the far end set up cones and left them there. One of the juniors worked spins by the gate and went off to physiotherapy. And around three o'clock the ice belonged to one person, and that person was working a step sequence in the gray light under the high windows, alone, at the far end, in the corner where the light came in cold.</p>

<p>Sasha put the book down flat across her lap.</p>

<p>Anna did the sequence once, without music, at the speed the music would have set. Then she did it again, slower. On the second pass the right shoulder held a hairsbreadth longer at the peak of the twizzle, and Sasha, whose eyes had spent fourteen years looking almost exclusively at the height of things, caught the correction, and understood that it was a correction, and understood further that no judge alive would have marked the difference and that Anna had marked it anyway, alone, at three in the afternoon, in an empty building, for nobody.</p>

<p>She did it a third time. The light came down through the high windows in long pale bars and lay across the ice, and Anna moved through the bars and out of them and into them again, small and precise and entirely unwitnessed, and the empty rink held all that gray brightness the way a room holds cold, generously, indifferently, on every surface, and gave none of it back to the girl on the bench.</p>

<p>So this was what it was, then. Sitting still. Sitting still was the thing she had spent her whole life outrunning, and it had caught her on a Thursday with a strapped ankle, and it had brought a friend to watch.</p>

<p>Something stood up in Sasha's chest, quietly, and did not sit down again.</p>

<p>It was not loud. That was what she could not get past afterward, lying awake: it was not loud, and everything in Sasha's life that had ever mattered had been loud. It was the sound a room makes when an appliance that has been running for years switches off, and only in the silence do you understand how much of the noise had been that, and how long it had been going, and that it had been going since a corridor and a tin of screws sorted by length.</p>

<p>She watched Anna Shcherbakova skate the same fifteen seconds of ice four more times, each one quieter than the last, and could not look away, and understood, without a single word for any of it, that not looking away had stopped being a reflex and had become a decision.</p>

<p>She did not jump that day. The count that day was zero.</p>

<p><em>Caesura.</em></p>

<p align="center">***</p>

<p>The ankle was fine by the following week. Everything else was not.</p>

<p>Fifteen came, and sixteen behind it, and with them came the cameras, which had always been at Khrustalny and had never before looked up. Now they looked up. They looked at the girl who jumped and the girl who did not miss, and they looked at the empty air between the two of them, and they decided, with the collective genius of people paid by the word, that the air was tense.</p>

<p>Trusova versus Shcherbakova. On every scoreboard, every website, every thumbnail. The Quad Queen and the Ice Princess. Two schools of thought on one team. Rivals from the same rink. Sasha had rolled her eyes at headlines her entire life on principle, and rolled them at these too, and the rolling had begun to cost something, the way a door costs something after a season of rain.</p>

<p>And underneath the headlines, in the place where the headlines were actually true, there was arithmetic, and the arithmetic was this: three.</p>

<p>Three places. One country, one Games, three places, and Khrustalny alone could have filled them twice over and had spares. Every girl in that building knew the number, and knew that the people who would take those places from her would not be foreigners in unfamiliar tracksuits but the girls whose birthdays she attended, whose skates she had borrowed, whose braids she had fixed in the two minutes before warm-up. Nationals would decide it. Nationals, and the two seasons of results leading into Nationals, and every single practice inside those two seasons, which meant every hour of every day was now an audition held against her own friends.</p>

<p>Nobody said any of that out loud. That was the thing Sasha found she could not forgive about the whole arrangement: it never once had to be said. It simply arrived in the building like a change in air pressure, and the girls who had shared canteen bread began, without one word of decision, to eat at separate tables.</p>

<p>The federation helped. Separate interview rooms, separate call times, separate photograph angles. When they were placed together for a two-shot they were placed at the correct distance for a headline, which was a distance Sasha noticed and did not correct, because correcting it would have meant explaining why she wanted to stand closer, and explaining why was not a service she offered even to herself.</p>

<p>The buses went on. That was the strangest part. They still traveled, still four hours out and four hours back with the windows fogging, and Anna still read, and somewhere in the middle of Sasha's sixteenth year the reading stopped being aloud.</p>

<p>There was no conversation about it. Neither of them announced a policy. There was simply, one evening on the road back from somewhere, a bus in the dark and a book open in the third row and no voice coming out of it, and Sasha sitting two seats back with her headphones in, and both of them looking out of their own windows at the same passing dark, and no reason available to either of them for why the seat between them had grown to two seats, and then to a row, and then to whatever it was now.</p>

<p>Sasha turned her head once, that night, to look down the aisle. Anna's face in the window's reflection, lit from below by the page. She did not turn around.</p>

<p>Four quads that week, all of them landed, one of them the best of her life. She counted them. They did not touch the thing standing in her chest. Nothing did.</p>

<p>Later, in a hotel room in a city she would not remember, Sasha lay in the dark with her phone under the pillow and took it out and made a note. A small figure of choreography: a wrist rotation with a hesitation in it, an entry that was not quite an entry, an ordinary and rather beautiful step that would go into next season's free skate and end, precisely, on the patch of ice where a second skater would have landed if there had been a second skater, if this had been a program for two, if the world had been arranged some other way.</p>

<p>She looked at it for a long time. Then she titled it, in her usual chaotic capitals, STEP, IT'S JUST A STEP, and put the phone back under the pillow, and lay listening to the building.</p>

<p>Three places. Two seasons. And a bus, somewhere out on a road between two cities, going home in the dark with its lights off and everyone asleep in it, and nobody reading aloud to anybody.</p>

<p><em>Dissolvendosi.</em></p>
