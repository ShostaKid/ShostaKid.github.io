<p align="center"><em>Allegro con fuoco</em></p>

<p align="center">***</p>

<p>The hair tie was unfamiliar.</p>

<p>It sat at the base of Sasha's ponytail, tighter than the ones she usually wore, plain and dark and without the elastic bounce her own tended to have by their third training day, and her hand had gone up to it three times since she had left the changing room, three times without meaning to, three times because her body, at seventeen, still trusted the parts of her that knew things her mind had not yet been asked to know.</p>

<p>She lowered her hand.</p>

<p>The corridor at the Capital Indoor Stadium was longer than corridors were supposed to be, and colder, and its walls had that specific institutional grey that arenas the world over have agreed on as the correct backdrop for waiting, and Sasha walked its length in her red Cruella dress, with her arms out at her sides for the cold, and she did not look at the two Chinese volunteers holding the door for her because she had learned, at fourteen, that meeting the eye of the door-holders makes the door feel like a country you are being deported from rather than one you are entering to fight in.</p>

<p>The corridor was long. The corridor was cold. The corridor ended in an ice surface she had waited for since she was eight.</p>

<p>She reached the door. Her hand went up to the hair tie again. She stopped it. She let it fall.</p>

<p><em>Ricordando.</em></p>

<p>The knock the night before had come at ten past eleven, which was late enough for a knock in Beijing to be a small event, and Sasha had been sitting on her bed with a book open that she was not reading and her hair loose around her shoulders and her phone face-down on the pillow, and the knock had been three short polite raps in a specific rhythm that Sasha had, without needing to think about it, recognized before she reached the door.</p>

<p>She opened it.</p>

<p>Anna stood in the hallway.</p>

<p>Anna in her ordinary hotel clothes, gray hoodie and black leggings, the hoodie's sleeve a familiar fraying she had watched grow across three seasons, and Anna's hair down for the first time in weeks, and Anna's face without the specific set it had worn every day of December, the set of a girl performing herself for an audience of cameras and coaches and shipping threads. Anna, in the hallway at ten past eleven with her hair down and no cameras present, looking exactly the way she had looked in October when she had eaten an orange from Sasha's hand at the boards.</p>

<p>"Can I come in."</p>

<p>Sasha, who was many things but was not, primarily, quick, said the first three-syllable thing she thought of.</p>

<p>"Of course."</p>

<p><em>Come prima.</em></p>

<p>The volunteers pushed the door open.</p>

<p>The arena hit her the way arenas hit her, all at once, the way weather hits, the sudden expansion of a small cold room into a large cold room with fifteen thousand people in it, and Sasha, whose whole life had been the ability to step into big rooms without shrinking, stepped in and did not shrink. She waved. She waved as if she meant it, because she did, because she had spent nine years learning how to make a wave land in the last row of the last balcony of every arena she had ever been in, and she made it land here. Camera flashes rolled a small pop across the seats like a distant surf. She skated to center.</p>

<p>Her music was not yet playing.</p>

<p>She stopped at her opening spot, arms low, breath deep, and she took, as she took at every competition, three full seconds to look at nothing before letting her body find the first shape, and in that three seconds her hand did not go up to the hair tie because her hand knew the cameras were there. But her mind did.</p>

<p>Her mind, without her permission, in the last of the three seconds, went back to the hallway.</p>

<p>Anna had come in.</p>

<p>She had come in the way she came into any room she was uncertain of, quietly, with a small extra pause at the threshold, and she had sat on the corner of Sasha's bed, not on the middle, not on the pillows, on the far corner, in the way of a person who had come only for a minute and did not want to be caught having stayed longer than a minute justified.</p>

<p>Sasha had sat on the other corner.</p>

<p>They had been three feet apart, on a hotel bed, in a room at the athletes' village at eleven at night on the eve of the ladies' Free, and neither of them had spoken for a length of time long enough that Sasha, in the ordinary run of her life, would already have broken it with a joke or a complaint or the specific mid-sentence pivot Sasha's silence always contained.</p>

<p>Sasha did not break it.</p>

<p>Sasha, who had not been in a room alone with Anna in six weeks, understood without needing to think about it that whichever of them spoke first would set the shape of what the room could hold, and that if she spoke first it would be a joke because that was what she was, and that a joke would close a door she had, without meaning to, watched Anna cross in order to be sitting on that bed.</p>

<p>So she waited.</p>

<p>Anna spoke.</p>

<p>"Your skates are drying wrong."</p>

<p>Sasha looked at her skates. Her skates were on the luggage rack, laces spread, blades up, exactly the way she always dried them.</p>

<p>"They are not."</p>

<p>"The left one is turned in. The moisture pools."</p>

<p>"It pools on the outside of the blade cover. It's fine."</p>

<p>"It's not fine. You have to turn it."</p>

<p>"Anichka."</p>

<p>"Turn it."</p>

<p>And Sasha, who had heard Anna say that word in that tone in that hotel room the way one hears a bell that has been waiting to be rung for the length of a whole winter, stood up, walked to the luggage rack, and turned her left skate the correct way, and sat back down on the bed, and said, in the voice she used only in rooms where cameras were not, "Happy?"</p>

<p>"Yes."</p>

<p>Sasha almost laughed.</p>

<p>She almost laughed, at eleven at night, in a hotel room in Beijing, at the girl she had not spoken to alone since November correcting the angle of her skate boots as if none of the winter had happened, and the almost-laugh sat in her chest for a beat, and then it did happen, small, unwilling, and Anna, on her corner of the bed, did not smile with her mouth but did with her eyes, and that, Sasha understood, was the mask beginning to come off.</p>

<p>The music hit.</p>

<p>Cruella opened with a bar that Sasha's body had, over the course of the season, come to recognize the way soldiers come to recognize their own war-cry, and her opening arm rose to meet it, and her spine snapped into the specific defiant line the choreography demanded, and she was moving before she had asked herself to move, and moving in the way she moved when the moving was not effortful, when the moving was the easiest thing in the world, when her body was doing what her body had been built to do for exactly this arena on exactly this night.</p>

<p>The first quad was the lutz.</p>

<p>She set the edge, drew the arm, threw. In the air she was the girl she had always been in the air, the girl no one at the boards could reach, the girl who had spent her whole life throwing her own body at gravity and winning. She rotated four times. She landed on a right blade of clean ice, flag up, run-out clean, and the arena, which had been holding its breath for the lutz because they had all read the same threads Sasha had read, let go of the breath in a single collective wall of sound.</p>

<p>Sasha did not celebrate.</p>

<p>Sasha kept skating. The next jump was coming. But under the noise, under the arena, under the music, under her own heart doing the specific fast thing it did between a landed jump and the next entry, she heard, exactly and precisely, the sentence Anna had said last night after the skate incident, and she had, in the hair tie at the base of her ponytail, the specific weight of what that sentence had meant.</p>

<p>After the skate incident, they had talked.</p>

<p>Not about the winter. Not about the threads. Not about Kamila and not about the coaches and not about the eight-word sentence at the boards in November. They had talked about, of all things, the breakfast at the athletes' village, which was bad, and the specific badness of the eggs, which were worse, and the woman at the coffee station who kept slipping Sasha extra almond milk in defiance of what appeared to be Russian federation policy on athlete beverages, and Anna had laughed, once, a real laugh, uncovered, at the almond milk woman, and Sasha, watching her, had understood the thing she had been unable to understand at any point in the previous three months.</p>

<p>The mask was a mask.</p>

<p>The girl who had smiled the Waltz smile in Sambo in December was the same girl who was, at eleven at night in Beijing, sitting three feet from her on a hotel bed, laughing at almond milk with her hair down. The mask went on when the cameras were there. The mask came off when the cameras were not. It was not a permanent installation. It was not a personality change. It was a coat Anna had put on to walk through a winter she had not chosen to walk through, and here in this room, with the door locked and the hallway empty, she had taken the coat off, and Sasha, who had spent all winter believing that the girl who had held her hand at Sambo was gone, understood that the girl was in this room now, three feet away, laughing.</p>

<p>Something in Sasha's chest did the thing it did when a jump landed cleanly.</p>

<p>She did not name it.</p>

<p>She did not need to.</p>

<p>Anna caught her looking. Anna's face did the small quick thing it always did when Sasha caught her at anything, and then Anna looked away, but Anna kept smiling, and Anna's smile, this smile, in this room, was not for a camera and was not for the eight-word sentence and was not a mask, and Sasha, sitting three feet away with her mind full of tomorrow's program and her body already halfway into it, saw the smile land the way a landed jump lands, whole, real, felt.</p>

<p>The flip.</p>

<p>She set it, threw it, rotated it, landed it, and the arena did what the arena had been doing all season for the girl who was throwing quads at it: the arena said yes. Sasha ran out clean, arm sweeping to the choreographed follow-through, and skated into the transition to the third jump.</p>

<p>The salchow.</p>

<p>She set it, threw it, rotated it, landed it, and the arena said yes again, and this time louder, because the arena had begun to understand, in the way arenas understand these things halfway through a program, what it was watching. It was watching a seventeen-year-old girl throw five quadruple jumps into a single Free Skate at the Olympic Games. It had never seen this before. It had, in some part of itself, known all season it was going to see it. It was seeing it now.</p>

<p>Sasha felt the arena understand.</p>

<p>Sasha, in the transition to her fourth quad, did the thing she had promised herself she would not do, which was to allow, for one bar, the belief that had been building in her since the almond milk laugh last night. The belief that this was working. That the five quads were going to be enough. That when the numbers came she would be on top of them and Anna would be second and the country would forgive them both for whatever the country had needed to be forgiven for, because a girl who won gold in Beijing was a girl who could not, by law of the country, be held accountable for having been an inconvenient friend. That she could carry both of them out of this year on the back of five landed quads. That the door Anna had walked through last night into her room would stay open now, on the other side of gold, and they would sit on beds again, and Kamila would laugh with them again, and the winter would be a thing that had happened to someone else.</p>

<p>She entered the fourth quad.</p>

<p>She threw it. She landed it.</p>

<p>The arena roared.</p>

<p>After the laugh, they had, for approximately four minutes, been the two people they used to be.</p>

<p>Anna had teased her about her red dress, which was on the chair, laid out for the morning, and had asked whether the wardrobe department had run out of subtlety or run out of fabric, and Sasha had said both, and Anna had laughed, and Sasha had said Anna had always been jealous of the dress, and Anna had said she had never in her life been jealous of anything red, and Sasha had said, low, careful, "Anneshka," and Anna had said, with the small mock-warning tone she used only for Sasha, "Sasha," and the winter, for four minutes, had stopped happening in the room.</p>

<p>Four minutes.</p>

<p>Sasha timed it later, in her memory, when she was trying to hold on to it. The clock on the bedside table had said 11:23 when the almond milk laugh had happened. The clock had said 11:27 when Anna had said, quieter, in the voice that meant she was about to leave, "I should go. You have to sleep."</p>

<p>"I know."</p>

<p>"Sashka."</p>

<p>"I said I know."</p>

<p>Anna stood.</p>

<p>The four minutes ended.</p>

<p><em>Con fuoco.</em></p>

<p>Sasha entered the fifth quad on the back straightaway with fifteen thousand people on their feet and her heart doing the specific thing hearts do in the last quarter of a program that has been landed clean, and she did not think about Anna. She did not think about the hair tie. She did not think about tomorrow or last night or the almond milk or any of it. She thought only about the edge and the arm and the count and the throw, because thinking about anything else in the last quarter of a program will lose you the program, and Sasha, at seventeen, in Beijing, had not spent nine years learning how to lose programs.</p>

<p>She set the edge.</p>

<p>She drew the arm.</p>

<p>She threw.</p>

<p>Four rotations. She wobbled at the third. She fought the fourth. She landed on a right blade that was not quite where she had asked it to be, and her free leg swung wide to catch the balance, and she caught it, and she kept skating, and the arena, which had watched the wobble and watched the catch, understood that she had done it, and the sound the arena made was not the wall of sound the earlier jumps had gotten. It was the specific narrower louder sound that arenas make when they have watched something they do not entirely have the vocabulary for.</p>

<p>Sasha, running out of the fifth quad with her free leg still finding its clean line, allowed herself, for one bar of music, the smile she had promised herself she would not smile until the ending pose.</p>

<p>She smiled it anyway.</p>

<p><em>Sospeso.</em></p>

<p>Anna had stood up.</p>

<p>Anna had stood up in the way people stand when they have made a decision they have not yet informed the room of, and she had walked to the door, and Sasha had followed her, and at the door Anna had done a specific thing.</p>

<p>She had taken the hair tie off her own wrist.</p>

<p>She had worn it there because her hair was down. She wore hair ties on her right wrist in the way girls do, hooked past the small bone, the way Anna's mother had worn hair ties, the way Anna had probably always worn them, and Sasha, at the door, in her red Cruella dress lying on the chair and her tomorrow lying past that, watched Anna take the hair tie off her wrist with the same two-fingered gesture Anna had used since she was eleven.</p>

<p>She held it out.</p>

<p>She did not explain it. She did not accompany it with words. She did not say "for tomorrow" or "for luck" or any of the specific hollow things she and Sasha had learned, in the ordinary run of competition life, to say to each other's shoulders before big performances. She held it out, and Sasha, understanding without being told, took it.</p>

<p>It was small. It was dark. It was warm from Anna's wrist.</p>

<p>Sasha closed her hand around it.</p>

<p>Then it happened, or almost happened, or came, in Sasha's memory later, to be the thing that had almost happened, which was that both of them had moved, at the same time, by the same fraction of an inch, forward. It was not a decision. It was not a plan. It was two bodies that had, over nine years, learned each other well enough that the movement toward the other was, in the geometry of doorways at eleven at night in Beijing, more or less inevitable.</p>

<p>They stopped at the same fraction of an inch.</p>

<p>Both of them stopped. Both of them stopped at the same fraction of an inch. Both of them stopped because both of them understood, without needing to discuss it, that a hug now would be a promise, and that the promise would be a thing neither of them was allowed by the following morning to keep, and that a hug given at eleven at night on the eve of the ladies' Free at the Beijing Winter Games between two girls one of whom was skating first and one of whom was skating second and both of whom were fighting for the same medal on the same country's coach's watch, was not, quite, a hug they were still permitted to be children enough to give.</p>

<p>So they did not.</p>

<p>Anna's hand went back to her side. Sasha's hand, holding the hair tie, went back to her side. Anna opened the door. Anna stepped through it. Anna did not look back, because looking back would have been the same thing as the hug they had just not given, and Anna had understood the same thing Sasha had understood, and had chosen the same thing.</p>

<p>The door closed.</p>

<p>Sasha stood in the hotel room holding a hair tie in her palm.</p>

<p>She put it on. She slept.</p>

<p><em>A tempo.</em></p>

<p>The step sequence was where Sasha lived.</p>

<p>The step sequence was where Cruella was Cruella and not just a run of jumps, where the dress became the character and the character became the girl and the girl became the arena, and Sasha's arms cut the air with the specific defiant flair the choreography demanded, and her feet found their edges in the specific quick pattern she had drilled every day since August, and she was not thinking now, she was doing, and the doing was the whole thing.</p>

<p>The final combination spin gathered itself under her.</p>

<p>She stepped into it. She centered. She spun. Her free leg came up into biellmann and her spine did what her spine had been asked to do since she was ten, and the arena watched the spin the way arenas watch spins, which is as an ending, because spins are how programs end and the audience of an arena at the Winter Games learns, over the course of two weeks, to feel a spin as the last word of a paragraph before the paragraph itself has finished.</p>

<p>She held it.</p>

<p>She released it.</p>

<p>She hit the ending pose.</p>

<p>Arms up. Chin lifted. The dress settling around her legs. Her chest heaving in the specific way chests heave after four minutes of trying to break physics for the country, and the arena did what she had spent her whole life waiting for arenas to do, which was to stand up and put every hand it had into the air and make, for one girl who had just landed five quads at the Olympic Games, the loudest noise it had made all night.</p>

<p>Sasha stood in the noise.</p>

<p>She held the pose because the choreography asked her to hold the pose. She held it for the count of eight because that was the count. And in the count her hand did not go up to the hair tie because her hand was above her head, and she could not have reached the hair tie if she had wanted to, and she did not need to, because she could feel it exactly where Anna had left it.</p>

<p>She dropped her arms.</p>

<p>She skated to the middle.</p>

<p>She turned, and bowed, and turned again, and her eyes, without her having asked them to, without her having decided to, without her permission, went to the tunnel on the far side of the rink where the next skater waited to go on.</p>

<p>Anna was there.</p>

<p>Anna in her Master and Margarita costume, warm-up jacket over it, one hand on the wall, doing the small last stretches Sasha had watched her do a thousand times in a thousand arenas, and Anna's face was, from this distance, the face it always was in the tunnel before a skate, quiet, focused, unreachable, the mask.</p>

<p>But Anna's eyes were on Sasha.</p>

<p>And when Sasha found them, across the arena, across the tunnel, across the winter and last night and the hair tie and the almost-hug, Anna smiled.</p>

<p>Small. Just the corner of her mouth. A quarter-inch of movement in a mask that did not otherwise move. A smile only a person who had spent nine years learning to read her face would have seen at all.</p>

<p>Sasha saw it.</p>

<p>Sasha smiled back.</p>

<p><em>Sereno.</em></p>

<p>The tie held.</p>
