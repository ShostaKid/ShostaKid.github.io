<p align="center"><em>Moderato, con moto perpetuo; e talvolta, sospeso</em></p>
<p align="center">***</p>

<p>The ice at six in the morning belonged to whoever was hungry enough to want it, and for the first four months of her life at Khrustalny that had been the new girl from Kazan, every day, without exception, because nobody had yet told her that the hour was supposed to be a punishment.</p>

<p>Kamila Valieva was fourteen and built like something the wind had made in a hurry, all wrist and ankle and a spine that could bend further than the physiotherapist considered reasonable, and she arrived in the building before the lights came up and stood in the tunnel mouth in the dark for a moment every single morning, not for any reason she could have explained, just to look at the sheet of it, whole and blue and unwritten, before she went out and started writing on it.</p>

<p>She warmed up fast because warming up was the toll you paid. Then she jumped.</p>

<p>Two fingers to the side of her throat before each one; that was the habit, so old she had stopped noticing it, laid there in the half-second before the entry the way another girl might touch a cross. The pulse under them ran quick and light and did not slow down for anything. It had never slowed down. At rest, on the bench, on the bus, asleep in the back of her mother's car with her mouth open, it ran on at a speed the team doctor had described as within range and had then, after a pause, described as within range for a bird, and Kamila had liked that so much she had made him say it twice.</p>

<p>She landed a triple, and then a harder triple, and then the thing she was not yet allowed to attempt in front of anybody, and got the landing at the cost of one hand touching down, and came out of it laughing at the empty building.</p>

<p>Nobody saw. That was the arrangement of the hour: it was hers, entirely, gloriously, and she spent it doing exactly what she wanted, which was going as fast as a human being can go without leaving the ground permanently.</p>

<p><em>Con moto perpetuo.</em></p>

<p align="center">***</p>

<p>The building woke at seven and arrived all at once, the way buildings do.</p>

<p>"Kamilochka!" This was audible from the corridor before the doors opened, which was how most sentences beginning with that name arrived. "Kamilochka, tell them. Tell them what I said yesterday about the salchow, because Alena is claiming I said the opposite and Alena is a liar."</p>

<p>"You did say the opposite," said Alena, coming through the doors first with her guards already off and her bag hitting the bench like a dropped argument. "You said, and I want this preserved for history, you said that anybody who thinks about the entry is already losing."</p>

<p>"That's the same thing."</p>

<p>"It is not remotely the same thing."</p>

<p>"Kamila. Kamilochka." Sasha arrived at the boards and put both hands on the rail and leaned in with the full weight of an appeal to a higher court. "You're the youngest. The youngest is honest. What did I say?"</p>

<p>Kamila, who had heard exactly what Sasha said yesterday and knew that Alena had quoted it correctly to the word, considered her position in the family and answered accordingly.</p>

<p>"You said," she said gravely, "that Alena thinks too much."</p>

<p>"There it is." Sasha threw her arms up. "There it is, from the mouth of a child."</p>

<p>"That is not what you said, you monster, and she's fourteen, she'll say anything to whoever fed her most recently, which was you, because I saw you give her half a chocolate bar in the corridor."</p>

<p>"It was a third of a chocolate bar and it was a bribe, and I stand by it."</p>

<p>This was what Kamila had come for. Not only the ice; she could have had ice in Kazan. This: the noise, the arguing, the four of them on one sheet at seven in the morning, the fact that the loudest and most famous girls in the country would waste ten minutes of their lives litigating a salchow in front of a fourteen-year-old and would call her Kamilochka while they did it. She had been in the building sixteen weeks and had been adopted somewhere around week two, the way a small determined animal gets adopted, which is to say without any formal decision being taken by anyone.</p>

<p>She looked around for the last of them.</p>

<p>Anna was already on the ice. She had come in at some point during the salchow trial without anybody noticing, which was a thing she did, and was at the far end running the pattern she always ran first, unhurried, laying it down edge by edge as though the ice needed convincing.</p>

<p>"Anichka!" Sasha bellowed, at a volume that made the light fittings consider their position. "Settle something."</p>

<p>"No."</p>

<p>"You haven't heard it."</p>

<p>"I heard all of it. I heard it from the corridor. I heard it in the car park." Anna came round the end of her pattern without breaking it. "Alena's right, you said it wrong, and Kamila lied to you for a third of a chocolate bar, which frankly is cheaper than I would have gone."</p>

<p>"Traitors," said Sasha, delighted. "This whole rink."</p>

<p>Kamila laughed so hard she had to put her hands on her knees, and when she straightened up Anna was already back into her pattern with her face perfectly composed, and had not looked up once during the entire performance, and Kamila filed that away with the small pleasure of somebody collecting a set.</p>

<p align="center">***</p>

<p>The comparisons started that autumn and never really stopped.</p>

<p>She has Sasha's engine. She'll do the quads before she's sixteen, watch. She's the next Trusova, only taller. The federation people said it in corridors with their voices lowered by exactly the amount that meant they wanted to be overheard, and the press said it out loud, and Kamila heard all of it and was not remotely troubled by any of it, because being the next Sasha was the plan. It had been the plan since Kazan. You did not move eight hundred kilometers from your mother's kitchen to be the next anybody quiet.</p>

<p>So she jumped. She jumped in the mornings and she jumped in the sessions and she jumped, once, in the corridor, in trainers, to prove a point to Alena, and got shouted at by a coach and by Alena and by the woman who cleaned the corridors, in that order.</p>

<p>And in between all of that, she watched Anna, and told herself she was studying technique.</p>

<p align="center">***</p>

<p>The humming she stole in November, on a Tuesday, off-ice, in the gym.</p>

<p>She had noticed it weeks before without registering it: that when Anna Shcherbakova was concentrating on something with her hands, taping a boot, threading a lace, adjusting the tension on a band, a sound came out of her. Not singing. Barely a sound at all. Five notes, low in the throat, climbing a little way and then stopping, going nowhere, folding back to the beginning and climbing again, and Anna did not know she was doing it. That was the extraordinary part. The most controlled person in the building leaked one small unguarded thing into the air, all day, every day, and had no idea.</p>

<p>Kamila, sitting cross-legged on the mat beside her with a band around her arch, listened to it for a while and then, being fourteen and possessing no filter of any kind, hummed it back.</p>

<p>The taping stopped.</p>

<p>"What was that?"</p>

<p>"You do it," Kamila said. "You've been doing it all morning."</p>

<p>"I have not."</p>

<p>"You did it in the corridor. You did it at the boards. You did it when you were fixing my blade guard on Thursday." Kamila counted them off on her fingers with enormous satisfaction. "You did it just now, twice, while you were taping. Five notes. It goes up and then it stops."</p>

<p>Anna looked at her with an expression Kamila had not seen on her before and would come to recognize over the following year as the specific face of a person discovering that they have been observed with more attention than they budgeted for.</p>

<p>"It does not go anywhere," Anna said, after a moment. "It's not a tune. It's nothing."</p>

<p>"It goes up and it stops," Kamila agreed happily. "Like the sixth one hasn't been invented yet."</p>

<p>And Anna, who had an answer for everything, went back to her taping without producing one, and did not hum again for the rest of the session, and Kamila sat on the mat with her band around her foot and the five notes going round in her head, pleased with herself in a way she could not have accounted for if anyone had asked.</p>

<p>She started using it that week, on ice, under her breath, as a count. It fit the way a stolen coat fits: badly at first, then not at all badly, then like something she had always owned. It was better than counting numbers, because numbers made her rush, and the phrase would not be rushed; it climbed at its own speed and stopped where it stopped, and she found her entries settling into it without her deciding.</p>

<p>Nobody heard it. It was too quiet for the boards and far too quiet for the seats. It was, for the entire winter, the only thing in Kamila Valieva's life that was not loud.</p>

<p align="center">***</p>

<p>The trouble started, as trouble does, with her getting exactly what she wanted.</p>

<p>She landed the quad toe in December, in a session, in front of everybody, and the rink went off, and Sasha screamed and hit the boards with both hands and Alena spun in a circle with her hands over her mouth and a coach said something into a telephone, and Kamila stood in the middle of it with her heart going at a speed no doctor would have described as within range for anything, and was, for about a week and a half, perfectly and completely happy.</p>

<p>Then, in January, on an ordinary evening at the end of an ordinary session, everybody went home and Anna stayed on.</p>

<p>Kamila had her boots off and one sock in her hand when she looked up through the glass and saw her still out there, alone, under the half-lights, running something slow.</p>

<p>There were no jumps in it. There was nothing in it a scoreboard would have thanked her for. It was a step sequence, or the bones of one, a long line of edges and turns laid down at a speed that would have bored Kamila to actual tears at any point in her previous fourteen years of life, and Kamila sat down on the bench with one sock on and one sock in her hand and did not move for eleven minutes.</p>

<p>She could not have said what she was looking at. That was the part that stayed with her afterward, when she tried to reconstruct the evening and failed: she had no vocabulary for it at all. There was a place, three-quarters of the way through, where Anna came out of a twizzle and simply held, arms low, nothing happening, for what could not have been more than a second, and the whole empty arena seemed to arrive at that spot with her and wait there, and then move on.</p>

<p>Kamila's own pulse, which never slowed for anything, slowed.</p>

<p>She put her hand to her throat out of pure habit and found it, quieter than she had ever felt it, and sat very still on a cold bench with one sock on, in a building emptying out around her, watching a girl three years older do nothing at all beautifully, and thought, with the sincerity of a fourteen-year-old who has never once in her life been asked to examine a feeling: I want to know how she does that.</p>

<p>She waited at the gate.</p>

<p>"Teach me that."</p>

<p>Anna, stepping off the ice, put her guards on before answering, which was the single most Anna thing available to her at that moment. "Teach you what?"</p>

<p>"The stop. In the middle. After the twizzle, you stop, and the whole building stops with you, and then you go on." Kamila had both hands out, describing shapes in the air, one sock still in her fist. "How do you do that? What's the count?"</p>

<p>"There isn't a count."</p>

<p>"There's always a count."</p>

<p>"Not for that." Anna picked up her bag and considered her, and something in her face came very close to amusement. "You can't get it from me. You'd have to want it, and you don't. You want the quad."</p>

<p>"I want both."</p>

<p>"Nobody wants both. Everybody says they want both." Anna started for the corridor, and then, over her shoulder, in the tone of a person handing over one single card off the top of the deck: "It's not a stop, anyway. That's why you can't count it. If you stop, it's dead. You have to still be going."</p>

<p>"That doesn't mean anything!"</p>

<p>"It means everything," said the girl with the chestnut hair, unhelpfully, from halfway down the corridor, "and you'll be furious about it for about a year," and was gone.</p>

<p>Kamila stood at the gate with one sock on.</p>

<p align="center">***</p>

<p>She was furious about it for eleven days.</p>

<p>She tried to copy it directly, which failed, because a copy of a held moment is just a girl standing on ice looking as though she has forgotten what comes next, and Sasha skated past during one of these attempts and asked, with genuine concern, whether she had hurt something.</p>

<p>She tried to build a count for it, which failed, because every number she put on it made it arrive too early or too late, and the harder she counted the more it turned into a gap in the program instead of a thing inside the program.</p>

<p>She tried, on the eleventh day, in the empty six o'clock hour, in the dark before the lights came up, doing nothing at all except skating slowly in a circle and being unbearably annoyed, and somewhere in the third circle she started humming without noticing, five notes, up and then stopping, going nowhere, and her pulse under her own two fingers came down to meet it.</p>

<p>And she understood, all at once, in the way fourteen-year-olds understand things, which is entirely and without any supporting evidence, that the phrase did not stop at the fifth note. It waited there. It was still going the whole time it was waiting, holding itself up in the air with everything running, and the sixth note had not been invented because the sixth note was not the point.</p>

<p>She put it into the pattern that morning. Right after the twizzle, where the ice opened up and the building was empty and nobody was watching: five notes, and the hold, and everything in her still going at full speed underneath while the surface of her did not move at all.</p>

<p><em>Sospeso.</em></p>

<p>She held it for one second and a half and came out of it and nearly fell over from the sheer illegal thrill of it, and skated to the boards laughing, and looked up.</p>

<p>Anna was in the tunnel mouth in her coat, bag on her shoulder, twenty minutes early for a session that started at seven.</p>

<p>She had watched the whole thing. She did not applaud, because she never did. She stood in the dark of the tunnel with her hands in her pockets for a moment longer, and then she said, in a voice pitched to carry exactly as far as the boards and no further:</p>

<p>"There. Now you want both."</p>

<p>And she went to change, and left the youngest girl in the building standing on the ice at ten to seven with two fingers at her own throat, counting a pulse that was running much too fast for a person who had just been standing still.</p>
