<p align="center"><em>Larghetto</em></p>

<p align="center">***</p>

<p>The Moscow winter began each morning before the sky agreed to it, and Anna arrived at Sambo-70 in that hour, when the streetlights were still on and the snow along the walkway to the arena caught the amber of them and threw it back up into the dark. She had made this walk so many mornings that her body knew the count of steps between the gate and the door, and her eyes could pick out without needing to try the one lamp that always flickered and the two windows in the coaches' offices already lit before hers. Sasha would be inside. Sasha was always inside first, though she lived farther and had by every measure more reason to be late, and that she was not was one of the small facts about her that Anna had learned without deciding to.</p>

<p>The doors closed behind Anna with the soft hush that all heavy doors make when they meet felt lining, and the rink air came to greet her in the way it always did, cool at the ankles and colder as it climbed, so that by the time she had crossed the lobby her breath was already showing. In the corridor a radiator ticked, patient as a clock that had all the time in the world. She passed the wall of framed photographs, older girls whose faces she had studied without recognition when she first came here at eleven, and she saw them now the way one sees furniture in one's own house, present without needing to be noticed. In the last photograph before the door to the rink, a girl was mid-flight with her arms crossed at her chest, and the light in the arena beyond made a corona through the glass that laid itself over the girl's white dress like a blessing given after the fact.</p>

<p>Anna pushed the door open.</p>

<p>Sasha was on the ice.</p>

<p>She was on the far side of the rink, at the boards, standing with one hand on the rail and one skate lifted behind her, testing her edge the way a person tests the temperature of a bath, without particular urgency, without any need to be observed. She was in her old warm-up jacket, the one with a sleeve fraying at the cuff that Eteri Georgievna had once threatened to burn, and her hair was pulled up in a way that suggested she had done it in the car with a hand mirror and given up. From this distance she looked smaller than she was. From this distance she also looked like nothing in the world could tire her, which was a lie that Anna had watched her tell many times, and which Anna, for reasons she had not asked herself to name, was always relieved to see her still able to tell.</p>

<p>Kamila was already at the bench.</p>

<p>She was sitting in the corner where the light from the high windows had not yet reached, and she was pulling her stretch band across the arch of her foot with both hands, slow, methodical, and she was humming. The humming was small, no louder than a person's inner voice made accidentally audible, and it was the same fragment of a phrase she had been humming for weeks now, five notes that climbed and then paused as if the sixth had not yet been invented. She did not look up as Anna passed. She did not need to. She hummed and pulled the band, hummed and pulled, and the phrase folded back on itself and began again.</p>

<p>Anna set her bag on the bench. The zipper of her jacket, when she pulled it, was a small metallic sigh in the quiet.</p>

<p>"You're late, Anichka," Sasha called across the ice, and her voice carried in the empty arena as if the arena had been made specifically to carry it, which perhaps it had.</p>

<p>"I'm two minutes early," Anna said, not looking up, sitting down to change her guards.</p>

<p>"You're two minutes late by my clock."</p>

<p>"Your clock has always been wrong."</p>

<p>"My clock knows what it wants."</p>

<p>Kamila hummed. The phrase climbed and paused. The phrase climbed and paused.</p>

<p>Anna finished her guards and stood, and Sasha, seeing her stand, pushed off from the boards without any preparatory glance, one long unhurried stroke, then another, then a third, coming across the ice in the way water comes when a channel is opened, unable to consider not coming. Anna watched her come. Anna had watched her come many times. There was a specific quality to the way Sasha moved across an empty rink that never quite existed when the rink was full, as if the emptiness gave her permission to be languid where she was otherwise all fireworks, and it was this Sasha, this before-anyone-else Sasha, that Anna kept a private inventory of, in the same way one might keep certain photographs in a drawer without needing to look at them, only knowing they were there.</p>

<p>Sasha stopped at the boards in front of her.</p>

<p>"Kamila's humming that thing again."</p>

<p>"She's been humming that thing for a month."</p>

<p>"It's going to lodge in my head and I'm going to skate my long program to it in Beijing."</p>

<p>"You skate everything to it. You just don't realize."</p>

<p>Sasha grinned. Sasha's grin was the whole arena's problem. It arrived without any concern for what it might do to the person receiving it, and it stayed for exactly as long as it wanted to, which was always a fraction of a second longer than seemed structurally sound. Anna had spent, over the years, a considerable amount of energy on not being obviously affected by it, and had achieved, over the years, a competence in this that she was almost proud of, in the way one is almost proud of a small survival skill one wishes one did not need.</p>

<p><em>Con tenerezza.</em></p>

<p>"Coach isn't here yet."</p>

<p>"She said seven."</p>

<p>"She said seven and she means quarter past. We have time."</p>

<p>"For what."</p>

<p>"For anything."</p>

<p>They stood at the boards. Beyond them the ice was pristine, resurfaced last night and left to set through the small hours, its surface so unmarked that in the low arena light it read almost blue, the blue of a shallow pool seen from above. Kamila's humming came from the bench behind them and reached the phrase's small unresolved pause, and held there, and began again.</p>

<p>"Come skate with me."</p>

<p>"We haven't warmed up."</p>

<p>"We're warmed up. Look at us. We're standing here talking. That's warm."</p>

<p>"That's not how warming up works."</p>

<p>"Come skate with me, Anneshka."</p>

<p>There was a pull to the word skate the way Sasha said it that had nothing to do with the ice, and Anna, who had spent years learning to hear it, put down her water bottle and stepped through the gate onto the fresh surface, and Sasha, having gotten what she wanted, executed the small victorious flourish she always executed on getting what she wanted, which involved skating backward for one long careless stroke and pretending not to be watching Anna follow her.</p>

<p>Kamila hummed. Whole notes. Sustained tones held under everything.</p>

<p>They circled the rink together in a wide slow arc, not touching, not speaking, and the world outside the arena walls receded until it was only the rink and the ice and the sound of their two blades laying down twin curves onto the untouched surface, one behind the other, then side by side, then converging where a bend brought them close and separating again where the geometry required. Anna could hear Sasha breathing. Anna could hear her own breath answer. It was a rhythm they had built without ever intending to, which was the only way, Anna had come to understand, that any rhythm worth having got built.</p>

<p>By the time they reached the far end of the rink, the light had begun to change. High in the arena's east wall, the row of long windows had lightened by a shade, and the amber of the streetlamps outside was surrendering to a paler blue that came in from a lower angle and laid itself across the ice in long soft trapezoids. Anna slowed to look at it. Sasha slowed with her. They stood at the boards on the far side and watched the light come, and the light came the way light comes when nothing has yet gone wrong, generously, indiscriminately, unconcerned with the particular girls it was touching.</p>

<p>"Look at that," Sasha said.</p>

<p>"I'm looking."</p>

<p>"No, look at it."</p>

<p>She was not, Anna realized, talking about the light. She was talking about the ice under the light, the way it took the pale blue and threw a paler blue back, the way the whole rink, in this one narrow minute before Eteri arrived and the day began, was capable of being beautiful in a way it would not be beautiful again until tomorrow at this exact hour. Anna looked at it. She looked at it for a long time. She was aware, as she looked, of Sasha standing beside her, close enough that Anna could feel the small heat of her through the winter layers between them, and she was aware, more distantly, that Kamila's humming had reached them from across the ice, thin and soft and steady, five notes climbing to a paused sixth that was still not there.</p>

<p>Then the door opened and Eteri came in and the day began.</p>

<p align="center">***</p>

<p><em>Poco meno mosso.</em></p>

<p>The cafeteria at Sambo-70 held that particular kind of after-training quiet, the kind Sasha had never gotten used to because it was too much like recovering from a fight in a room where the fight was already over. She was at the corner table with her feet up on the opposite chair, chewing a folded piece of black bread and cheese she had built badly, and she was watching Anna, and she was not entirely admitting to herself that this was what she was doing.</p>

<p>Anna was at the counter waiting for tea behind an older man who was fighting a coffee machine, and she had not offered to help him. She stood at a distance from him that Sasha understood was a distance she would have to cross whole cities to cross herself, because it was the distance of a person who had been raised to consider that one's presence, given without being asked for, was a burden. Sasha had never in her life considered that her presence might be a burden. This was, Sasha suspected, why Anna had always been the more polite of them and Sasha had always been the more loved.</p>

<p>The distinction was fine and Sasha, when she was tired enough to be honest, understood that it hurt her.</p>

<p>Anna's shoulders were hunched. The hoodie she had put on was too big for her by half a size, and its color, a soft gray-green like the underside of a leaf, was not a color Anna had ever bought for herself, which meant Anna had picked it up from a bag or a bench or a locker where someone had left it, and Sasha, in the same movement of thought, understood two things: one, that the hoodie was hers; two, that Anna almost certainly knew this. She had put it on anyway. She had also, when she had put it on, not looked to see if Sasha was watching.</p>

<p>Sasha, mouth full of bad bread and cheese, kept watching.</p>

<p>Kamila was two tables away with a bowl of something and a book. She had eaten half of the something and read approximately none of the book, and her humming had softened, in the after-training quiet, into something wordless, no clear notes, only the shape of the notes, a suggestion of a phrase without the phrase's insistence. It came and went at intervals in the room as if she were breathing it rather than choosing to hum it, and Sasha, who noticed most things distantly, noticed this: that Kamila was the only one of the three of them who had ever hummed at Sambo, and that everyone had accepted it, and no one had ever asked her to stop, and no one had ever asked her what it was.</p>

<p>Anna reached the counter. Anna pushed a button. The machine cleared its throat.</p>

<p>Sasha, whose whole life had been the ability to look at something for exactly as long as she wanted, looked at Anna for exactly as long as she wanted.</p>

<p>The hoodie fit her wrong at the shoulders. The color did not suit her, exactly, but did not disagree with her either. She stood at the counter with both hands wrapped around the paper cup as it filled and she was already breathing the steam up into her face for warmth. Her hair, at the ends, had begun to curl the way it did when the shower water had been colder than it should have been. Sasha had asked her once why she did not turn it hotter. Anna had said something at the time that Sasha could no longer remember, and had watched Anna and not the answer, and had missed it.</p>

<p>She missed a lot of Anna's answers this way.</p>

<p><em>Sospeso.</em></p>

<p>Anna turned from the counter with her tea. She looked across the room. She saw Sasha at the corner table with her feet up and her bread in her hand, and she did the thing Anna always did in the second before she started walking, which was to stand still for a beat as if to check that walking was, in fact, the correct next move. Then she walked. She crossed the cafeteria at her ordinary pace, not looking at anyone, not looking at Kamila, not looking away from Sasha either. She reached the table. She set down the tea. She sat.</p>

<p>She did not say anything about the hoodie.</p>

<p>Sasha did.</p>

<p>"You steal from lockers now, Anichka."</p>

<p>"It was on the bench."</p>

<p>"It was on my bench."</p>

<p>"Then technically it was on the bench of the person who left it there before I did."</p>

<p>"You know exactly whose bench it is."</p>

<p>"I know it's warm. Are you giving me a lecture on property law before nine, after a five-hour session, with hair I haven't dried."</p>

<p>Sasha did not have anything to say to this, because Anna, when she had slept badly and trained hard and put on someone else's hoodie without meaning to admit whose it was, produced sentences that closed like doors, and the only thing left to do with those doors was to lean against them. Sasha leaned. Sasha picked up her bread. Sasha kept looking.</p>

<p>"How's your ankle."</p>

<p>"There's nothing wrong with my ankle."</p>

<p>"There is something wrong with your ankle."</p>

<p>"You have not been near my ankle, Anneshka."</p>

<p>"I have eyes."</p>

<p>Sasha put down her bread. She looked at Anna. Anna was blowing on her tea and looking at Sasha over the rim of the cup with the specific patient expression she used when she was waiting for Sasha to stop lying about something they both knew Sasha was lying about, and this expression, which Sasha had at various points in her life responded to with defiance, with humor, with cheerful invention, and with silence, tonight, for reasons Sasha did not want to look at directly, made her want to put her head down on the table and go to sleep, and did not make her want to lie.</p>

<p>"It's fine," she said. "It's a little tight after the second run-through. Iced it last night. Fine now."</p>

<p>Anna held the tea. Anna waited.</p>

<p>"Fine, Anichka. Really. It'll hold."</p>

<p>Anna did not answer this. Anna took a small careful sip of her tea, and then set the cup down between them, and then, in the smallest gesture, so small that if Sasha had looked away for a second she would have missed it, moved the cup an inch closer to Sasha's side of the table. Not offered. Placed. In a location where, if Sasha wanted a sip, she could have one, and where, if Sasha did not want one, the tea would sit and cool and no one would need to talk about it.</p>

<p>Sasha looked at the tea.</p>

<p>"You could just say drink it."</p>

<p>"You'd drink it because I told you to?"</p>

<p>"No."</p>

<p>"Then there's no reason to say it."</p>

<p>The steam came off the tea in a thin translucent column and dissolved into the cafeteria air a hand's width above the cup, and it kept going up even as it disappeared, the way a person keeps walking even after they have stopped being visible in a crowd, and Sasha found that she was, without having decided to, watching the place where the steam went away, as if the fact of it not being there anymore was more interesting than the fact of it having been there in the first place.</p>

<p>Kamila hummed. The shape climbed. The sixth note did not arrive.</p>

<p><em>Sospeso.</em></p>

<p>The room did what it always did between sessions, which was to hum with the low mechanical sound of the coffee machine and the higher irregular sound of two athletes at another table arguing about something in a magazine, and none of this touched their corner. Their corner was a still pool inside the noise, and Sasha, who had always lived at the loud end of every room she had ever been in, understood without knowing how she understood it that this small still pool was not something the room had made. She had made it. Anna had made it. Between them, without discussion, they had built a place in the cafeteria where the noise could not reach, and they were sitting inside it, and they were not looking at each other now, and neither of them was going to move first, because to move first would be to acknowledge that the pool existed, and to acknowledge that the pool existed would be to acknowledge who had built it and why.</p>

<p>Sasha reached for the tea.</p>

<p>She took a sip.</p>

<p>It was too hot. She swallowed it anyway.</p>

<p>She set the cup back down at the exact place Anna had put it, not one inch closer to herself, not one inch farther, and she picked up her bread and cheese, and she resumed eating as if nothing had happened, because nothing had happened, because they had agreed, without agreeing, that nothing would happen, and Anna, who had watched her drink and watched her put the cup back and watched her go back to her bread, said only, in the same tone she used for the weather:</p>

<p>"You'll wear the ankle brace tomorrow."</p>

<p>"I'll wear it."</p>

<p>"Thank you."</p>

<p>Kamila hummed. The phrase climbed. The sixth note did not arrive. The sixth note, Sasha suspected, was not going to arrive today, was not going to arrive tomorrow, was not going to arrive at Beijing or after Beijing, and the fact that Sasha had begun to think this way about a small phrase her younger training-mate was humming across the room was, in itself, a fact that she was not, tonight, in her tiredness, in her small warm shame at the hoodie and the tea, prepared to examine.</p>

<p>Outside the high windows of the cafeteria, the afternoon light had gone from silver to something like the color of pewter, and the snow that had begun to fall an hour ago was falling harder now, and the room, without anyone noticing, had become slightly colder.</p>

<p align="center">***</p>

<p><em>Adagio.</em></p>

<p>By the time evening session ended it was already night outside, though it had been night in some sense since three, and the three of them, without deciding to, had ended up back at the rink after the others had left, which was not unusual for that winter. Eteri had gone home. The junior group had gone home. The building was that particular kind of empty that a large sports facility becomes after dark, the kind where the ventilation carries and every distant door sounds close.</p>

<p>Kamila had asked. Kamila always asked. She had asked in her small careful voice whether they might stay just a little longer, only a little longer, because she wanted to try the step sequence one more time, only once, only until she got the timing right, and Anna had said yes and Sasha had said yes and both of them had known before saying yes that neither of them had wanted to leave either, that Kamila's asking had been permission for a wanting that was, in each of them, older than she was.</p>

<p>The rink was on its low lights.</p>

<p>The overhead panels had been switched off an hour ago and only the perimeter lights remained, warm and low, the way the last light of a house looks from the outside when almost everyone in it has gone to sleep. The ice, under this light, was no longer the pale blue of morning. It was the color of a pewter plate, and its surface, cut and re-cut by the day's blades, held the shape of every jump every person in the group had failed to land, small parallel scars laid over one another until the whole thing was a document of what had been survived.</p>

<p>Kamila was skating slow figure-eights in the far corner.</p>

<p>She was not doing her step sequence. She had done it three times and had not liked any of them, and she had wandered off into figure-eights the way she always did when she was thinking, and she was humming. The humming was different now. In the morning it had been the phrase. In the afternoon it had been the shape of the phrase. Now, tonight, alone in a rink lit like a candle, it was only two notes, held long, the first one for a long slow count and the second one shorter, and the second one bent slightly at its ending, as if she were feeling for a resolution she could not find and was not sure she wanted to.</p>

<p>Anna sat on the boards. Sasha sat beside her, close enough that their coats touched.</p>

<p>Neither of them said anything.</p>

<p>Below them Kamila drew her figure-eights on the pewter ice, and each figure-eight closed on itself and began again, and each closed again and began again, and Kamila's humming rose and fell over her movement without ever quite meeting it, the way a person sings a song their body already knows without needing to sing on the beat. The rink air was very cold. The high windows above them had gone black. Somewhere in the building a radiator ticked its unhurried tick, and it was the same tick as the morning tick, which meant the radiator had been ticking through the whole day, patient as a clock that had all the time in the world.</p>

<p>Sasha shifted her weight and her shoulder pressed briefly against Anna's, and Anna, who had known the shift was coming, did not move away.</p>

<p>They watched Kamila skate.</p>

<p>"She's tired," Sasha said, very quietly.</p>

<p>"She's not tired. She's fourteen. She's thinking."</p>

<p>"She looks tired."</p>

<p>"That's because you look at her the way you look at me."</p>

<p>Anna had not meant to say this. Anna had said it because she was, at that hour, in that light, sitting on those boards with Sasha's shoulder against her own and the whole long day behind them and the whole longer year ahead, incapable of the small necessary work of not saying it, and she watched the words settle in the air between them the way a person watches a glass they have knocked over settle onto the table without breaking.</p>

<p>Sasha did not answer immediately.</p>

<p>Below them Kamila closed a figure-eight and began the next one. The two notes rose and fell. The one that bent at its ending bent again.</p>

<p>"I don't know how I look at you," Sasha said, at last, into the cold air.</p>

<p>"You don't have to."</p>

<p>"I might have to."</p>

<p>"Not tonight."</p>

<p>The word tonight sat between them. Anna did not know why she had said it. She had said it because there was a difference between the sentence they were now inside of and the sentence they had been inside of at seven that morning at the boards on the far side of the rink, and the difference had a shape she did not want to name and could not quite avoid, and the shape was the shape of the sixth note in Kamila's phrase that had never yet arrived. It was going to arrive. Anna understood, sitting there, that it was going to arrive, and that when it did the piece would end, and that she was not, tonight, prepared to hear it.</p>

<p>Sasha's shoulder pressed a little harder against her own.</p>

<p>Kamila hummed.</p>

<p>Anna, without looking at Sasha, without turning her head, without doing anything visible to the empty arena or to Kamila or to whatever version of herself was going to have to survive tomorrow, moved her gloved hand along the boards and let it rest against Sasha's gloved hand, and Sasha, without looking at her, without turning her head, without doing anything visible to anyone, turned her hand and closed her fingers around Anna's, and they sat like that, on the boards, in their coats, above the pewter ice, above Kamila's figure-eights, above the unresolved phrase, above the year they had left, and neither of them said the thing, because the thing did not need saying, because Kamila was singing it, quietly, in two notes, for both of them, into a rink built to carry any voice at all.</p>

<p><em>Molto legato.</em></p>

<p>Outside, the snow had begun again.</p>
