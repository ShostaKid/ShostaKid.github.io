<p align="center"><em>Tempo di ciaccona, a tre voci, come neve che finalmente si posa</em></p>

<p align="center">***</p>

<p>Morning had always been Anna's least favorite time of day, and so it took her a long moment, lying still in the blue half-light of late December, to understand what was different about this one.</p>

<p>Sasha was still there.</p>

<p>She lay sprawled across her half of the bed and a generous portion of Anna's, one arm flung over her head, red hair scattered across the pillow like something spilled and unregretted, sleeping the way she did everything, loudly, completely, taking up all the room she was given and then a little more. For years Anna had woken to the cold shape of her absence and the sounds of breakfast being invented in the kitchen. Today, for once, Anna had woken first, and she lay on her side and watched her lover breathe, and she understood that this small reversal was not an accident. Some part of her had set its own alarm. Some part of her had wanted to be the one who watches, this morning, before the day asked of her the thing it was going to ask.</p>

<p>She slipped out from under the blanket carefully, the way you leave a room where something precious is balanced on the edge of a shelf, and went to the bathroom.</p>

<p>The mirror was the same mirror. Two weeks ago she had stood in front of it with a toothbrush full of foam and a pain in her jaw and no idea that the day would end with three years of buried winter sitting across from her in a white coat. Anna leaned on the sink and looked at herself, and her tongue went, on its own, to the temporary filling on the upper right, the smooth foreign patch it had returned to fourteen days in a row, quietly, compulsively, the way the mind returns at two in the morning to the one thing it has promised itself not to touch. Two weeks of touching the patch. Three years of touching the other thing beneath it.</p>

<p>Today the temporary came out, and something permanent went in. That was all the appointment card said. Anna looked at the girl in the mirror, and the girl in the mirror raised her fingers, briefly, to her own lips, the old unconscious gesture, the residue of a night that had never finished happening, and Anna watched her do it and lowered her hand deliberately, like setting down a glass.</p>

<p>Enough, she thought. A tooth left rotting reaches the pulp. She had learned that much, two weeks ago, lying in a chair with her fears held gently by the last person on earth she would have chosen to hold them.</p>

<p>In the kitchen, the smell of coffee had preceded its maker; Sasha shuffled in three minutes later, half awake, wearing the expression of a woman personally betrayed by the concept of morning.</p>

<p>"You're up," Sasha said, suspicious. "Voluntarily. Before me. Should I check the window for a second sun?"</p>

<p>"The appointment is at ten."</p>

<p>"I know. I'm driving you." Sasha was already reaching for the pan, for the eggs, for the day's command. "We'll leave at nine thirty, the roads will be..."</p>

<p>"Sasha." Anna wrapped both hands around her coffee. "I want to go alone."</p>

<p>The pan paused, a centimeter above the stove. It was a very small pause, and in anyone else it would have meant nothing, but Anna had spent years learning this woman's pauses the way Sasha had spent years learning her walls, and she watched the words land, and be understood, entirely understood, more of them than Anna had actually said out loud.</p>

<p>"Alone," Sasha repeated.</p>

<p>"You would sit in the parking lot for an hour terrifying the pigeons. Go for your run. I'll take the metro."</p>

<p>Sasha set the pan down and looked at her for a moment longer, and there was something in the blue of her eyes that Anna could not fully read, something that looked strangely like the moment before applause. Then it folded itself away, and Sasha grinned, and the morning resumed.</p>

<p>"Fine. But who are you, and what have you done with the girl who once bit a dentist?"</p>

<p>"I was seven."</p>

<p>"He probably still tells the story at conferences. The Shcherbakova Incident." Sasha cracked two eggs with one hand, entirely too pleased with herself. "Text me when it's done. And no chewing on the right side until she says so, I know you, you'll be eating sukhariki by noon out of pure spite."</p>

<p>Anna laughed, and finished her coffee, and put on her coat, and at the door Sasha caught her by the sleeve and kissed her, unhurried, on the left cheek, the safe side, with the precision of a woman who had memorized exactly where it hurt.</p>

<p>"Go on then," Sasha said softly. "Brave girl."</p>

<p>The street outside was deep in New Year already. Fir garlands climbed the lampposts, and strings of lights hung between the buildings with their bulbs still on in the gray morning, pale gold against the snow that had been falling on Moscow, off and on, for two weeks, ever since the night of the first appointment. The whole city was leaning toward the holiday the way a room leans toward a lit stove. Anna walked to the metro through the middle of all that gathering warmth, and her heart was beating hard, and she let it. Fear, she had decided somewhere around dawn, could come along if it wanted. It just wasn't allowed to drive.</p>

<p align="center">***</p>

<p>Kamila Valieva had not counted the fourteen days. She wanted that noted, formally, in whatever ledger the heart keeps of its own defeats: she had not counted them, because counting them would have meant admitting they were being counted, and so instead she had simply known, at every moment of every one of them, exactly how many were left, which her discipline insisted was a different thing entirely.</p>

<p>At 9:52 the nurse put her head around the door and said, "Your ten o'clock is here. Shcherbakova." A pause, freighted with the particular curiosity of front-desk staff. "She came alone."</p>

<p>Alone. Kamila finished writing the previous patient's notes in her controlled, forward-leaning hand, and did not look up, and said, "Send her in when she's ready," and inside her chest something turned over slowly, once, like a body changing position in its sleep.</p>

<p>Anna came in with her chin up. It was, Kamila recognized, the courage of the prepared: she had clearly rehearsed the walk from door to chair, and she executed it well, hanging her coat herself, sitting without being told, folding her hands in her lap. Only her eyes moved too quickly, cataloguing the instruments on the tray, and Kamila found the sight of that catalogued fear almost unbearably familiar, because she had spent her whole life doing the same thing with a different set of instruments.</p>

<p>"The temporary held?" Kamila asked, pulling on her gloves.</p>

<p>"I did everything you said. Left side only. The medication after meals."</p>

<p>"Good." Kamila adjusted the lamp, angling it off the girl's eyes, the small mercy of two weeks ago repeated without comment. "This visit is simpler than the last. I remove the temporary filling, place the permanent restoration, and check the bite. There is no drilling into anything living today. If you need me to stop, raise your left hand."</p>

<p>"I remember the system."</p>

<p>She worked. Her hands did what ten thousand hours had built them to do, and the room was quiet except for the small sounds of the work, and Anna lay still, her breathing better than last time, her left hand open on the armrest, and the chestnut hair spilled across the white headrest exactly as it had spilled two weeks ago and five years ago and in every one of the fourteen nights Kamila had not counted. The permanent filling went in. The blue light cured it, a small toy star held against the tooth, ten seconds, ten more. Bite check. Adjustment. Bite check again.</p>

<p>"Done," Kamila said, and turned away to the desk, and began the notes. "The restoration is permanent. It will outlast the tooth around it if you take care of it. Which," and the old dryness surfaced before she could file it down, "history suggests is not guaranteed, Shcherbakova."</p>

<p>Behind her, she heard Anna sit up. Heard her not stand. Heard, in the silence where the sounds of leaving should have been, the sound of a person assembling something.</p>

<p>"Kamila."</p>

<p>Not Doctor Valieva. Kamila's pen stopped mid-word. She had waited five years to hear her own name in that voice, directed at her, only at her, and now that it had arrived she discovered that all her preparation was for the wrong disaster.</p>

<p>"Three years ago," Anna said. "The sofa."</p>

<p>The pen was set down. Kamila turned in her chair, slowly, and made her face into the face that delivered diagnoses, and looked at the girl sitting on the edge of the dental chair with her hands gripping her own knees like a schoolchild about to recite.</p>

<p>"Patients say strange things after procedures," Kamila offered quietly. It was a door held open. It was the last kindness she had in stock: an exit, gift-wrapped as a dismissal.</p>

<p>"I'm not under anesthetic." Anna did not take the door. Her voice was uneven, and she went on anyway, in pieces, the way you carry something too heavy in one trip because you know you will not come back for a second. "It was dark. I was half asleep, and your hair, before Sasha dyed hers, in the dark it was... I thought you were her. You know what I thought. And afterward I said nothing, for three years, to anyone. I let you move out. I let your name become a stranger's name in my own kitchen. I built a whole city block around you just to avoid walking down one street." Her tongue touched, briefly, the new permanent thing in her tooth; her hand rose halfway to her lips and stopped, and came down. "I'm sorry, Kamila. Not for the mistake. The dark did that. I'm sorry for the three years."</p>

<p>The room was very quiet. Outside the window, the snow that had been falling on Moscow all December went on falling, unbothered, filling in every footprint the morning had made.</p>

<p>"You reached for someone you love," Kamila said at last, and each word was placed the way she placed instruments, exactly, in the tray built for it. "It was dark, you were half asleep, and you reached in the only direction you know. I have had three years to examine the event from every angle, and I have never once, not for a single night, mistaken it for anything else." Not for a single night. The truest sentence she had ever constructed, and its truth ran in a direction Anna could not see, and Kamila watched it arrive as pure absolution and let it be only that. Some medicines work best when the patient does not know everything they contain.</p>

<p>Anna's eyes had filled. She nodded, twice, wet and businesslike, a girl accepting a verdict better than the one she had rehearsed for.</p>

<p>"There's another thing," she said. "You stopped. That night. You were drunk, and it was dark, and no one on this earth would ever have known, and you stopped anyway." She looked up. "I was too much a coward to say anything for three years, so I'm saying all of it now. Thank you for stopping."</p>

<p>Kamila sat very still, in the stillness of someone working hard at it, and thought about a promise made on a Sunday afternoon that smelled of black tea and turpentine, and about how strange it was to be thanked for the single worst night of her life, and how the thanks did not hurt the way she would have predicted. It landed somewhere that had been standing empty. It landed, and stayed, and the room did not break.</p>

<p>"You weren't mine to not stop for," she said simply. "There was nothing else to do."</p>

<p>Anna stood then, and gathered her coat, and at the door she paused with her hand on the frame, and when she turned around there was something in her face that Kamila remembered from a Tuesday evening in October, five years ago, an unconditional warmth pointed directly at her, the warmth of a girl who greeted people as though she had been waiting for them.</p>

<p>"One more thing. That November, in the dorm. I fell asleep on the sofa waiting for Sasha, and someone put a blanket over me. Sasha never remembered doing it."</p>

<p>A long pause.</p>

<p>"The dorm was cold," Kamila said.</p>

<p>"Mm." Anna smiled, small and slow, and did not push the door of that answer any further open, and Kamila understood that she had seen through it, one corner of it, just enough, and had decided to let the rest stay folded. "Sasha makes Olivier for New Year. Industrial quantities. Nobody has ever finished it, in the entire history of the dish. December thirty-first, our place."</p>

<p>"I don't work New Year's Eve," Kamila said, which was not yes, and was yes, and both of them heard it correctly.</p>

<p>"I know. Sasha still has your number."</p>

<p>Then she was gone. The footsteps went down the corridor, lighter than two weeks ago, steady, unaccompanied, and Kamila listened until she could not hear them, and then she listened to the silence that followed, and found that it had changed its composition entirely. It was still the silence of a room someone has just left. But for five years, that silence had been a sealed jar, and now, somewhere in its glass, there was a hairline opening, and through it, faintly, came the smell of a kitchen on the thirty-first of December.</p>

<p>Her hand rose, halfway to her lips, the old orbit, the three-year habit.</p>

<p>She looked at it. Then she lowered it, laid it flat on the desk beside its twin, and sat for a moment with both palms against the cool laminate, steady, and reached for the appointment book, and wrote nothing in it, because there was nothing more to treat, and turned instead to the small calendar by the lamp and looked, for a while, at the last day of the year.</p>

<p align="center">***</p>

<p>Sasha did not go for a run.</p>

<p>She had said she would, and she put on the running shoes, and then she stood in the hallway of the apartment for a full minute, keys in hand, doing the math of it: eleven minutes to drive to the clinic, four to find parking, and then an hour of sitting in the car like a bodyguard, watching a door, guarding a girl from a danger that was three years old and had never once been a danger at all. She took the shoes off. She put the keys back in the bowl. Letting go, she was discovering, was not a decision you made once in the morning. It was a decision the morning kept asking you to make again, every quarter hour, in smaller and pettier forms.</p>

<p>So she cooked.</p>

<p>She took the steaks out of the freezer, the same cut she had been halfway through frying two weeks ago when a cry from the bathroom pulled her off the stove, and it seemed to her fitting, in a way she felt no need to explain to anyone, that the meal interrupted by all of this should be the one waiting at the end of it. She seasoned them properly this time. No hurry. The kitchen filled slowly with warmth and the sound of fat learning its lesson in the pan, and the window above the sink went on framing its rectangle of falling snow, and Sasha worked, and waited, and did not check her phone more than once every ten minutes, which for her was a monastic achievement.</p>

<p>She had spent three years waiting like this, in a manner of speaking. Waiting without pushing. She had thought of it, always, in the language her body understood: you can talk a person onto the ice, you can even hold them through the entry, but there is a point in every jump where your hands have to come off, where the rotation is theirs alone, and whatever you have given them before that moment is all you get to give. Two weeks ago she had removed the detour. Today she had taken her hands off. The rest was Anna's air, Anna's axis, Anna's landing, and all Sasha could do was what every coach in history has done at that moment: stand at the boards, and watch, and keep her face calm, and burn.</p>

<p>The text came at 11:40. <em>Done. Tooth is permanent now. So is something else. Coming home.</em></p>

<p>Sasha read it four times. Then she put the phone down, and turned the steaks, and smiled at the pan for a while in the empty kitchen, because there was no one there to see her do it and some things want to be done unobserved.</p>

<p>Anna came home with snow in her chestnut hair and her cheeks raw from the cold, and Sasha read her from the doorway in one long practiced glance, the way she had read her after every exam and every result and every closed door of their shared life, and found, for the first time in three years, no wall standing anywhere on the property. Just tiredness, the good kind, the kind that follows spent courage. Just her girl, home hungry.</p>

<p>"Steak," Anna said, with reverence, unwinding her scarf.</p>

<p>"Left side only. She said the bite needs a day to settle, I assume."</p>

<p>"She did say that." Anna hung her coat, came into the kitchen, stole a piece of bread, was permitted. Then, with a studied casualness that would not have deceived a passing stranger, let alone the woman holding the spatula: "I invited Kamila for New Year."</p>

<p>The steaks hissed. Sasha reached for the salt.</p>

<p>"Then I'll buy more potatoes," she said.</p>

<p>And that was all, and Anna looked at her for a moment with an expression that suggested she had prepared, on the metro, an entire diplomatic briefing for this negotiation and was now holding it, unopened, like an umbrella on a sunny day, and then she laughed, a little helplessly, and set the table.</p>

<p align="center">***</p>

<p>That night, in the dark, in the warm ordinary dark of a bed shared so long that even the mattress had taken sides, Anna lay with her back against Sasha's chest and Sasha's arm heavy over her waist, and stared at the strip of streetlight on the ceiling, and said:</p>

<p>"Sasha."</p>

<p>"Hm."</p>

<p>"Three years ago. The night before I got a stomach ache that wasn't a stomach ache." She felt the arm around her stay exactly where it was, neither tightening nor withdrawing, holding its weight steady like a hand kept flat on a frightened animal. "It was Kamila on the sofa. I thought she was you. It was dark, her hair, back then... I kissed her, Sasha. Believing she was you. And then in the morning I understood what I'd done, and I ran away, and I never told you. Not because of the kiss. The kiss was the dark's fault. I never told you because I was a coward, and the longer I didn't, the bigger the not-telling grew, until the not-telling was the whole crime and the kiss was just its excuse. So. Now you know."</p>

<p>The room breathed. Beyond the window the snow was still coming down, thick now, unhurried, the streetlight catching each flake for a half-second of gold on its way to the ground.</p>

<p>"I know," Sasha said.</p>

<p>Anna went very still. "...You know."</p>

<p>"Anya. My love. My clever, careful girl." Sasha's voice in the dark was low and warm and faintly, fondly amused, the voice of someone finally setting down a suitcase at the end of a very long platform. "You came out of that bedroom the next morning, and you looked at Kamila on the sofa, and every drop of blood left your face, and you touched your own mouth like it belonged to someone else, and then you invented the least convincing stomach ache in the history of medicine and fled the building. I was standing right there. Holding eggs. I've known since the eggs, dorogaya. Three years."</p>

<p>"Three years." Anna's voice came out very small. "You knew for three years and you never said one word."</p>

<p>"What word? There was no word that helped. If I asked, you'd have had to lie to me or confess to me before you could survive either one, and I wasn't going to do that to you to make myself more comfortable." A pause; the arm tightened now, finally, gathering her in by one degree. "It was yours to say. I just kept the door open until you could walk through it. You want to know a secret? Some jumps you can't throw a person into. They have to enter alone, or it doesn't count."</p>

<p>Anna lay in the dark doing the arithmetic of it, the whole three years recalculating themselves at once: every time Kamila's name had crossed the kitchen and been let pass, every question never asked, every micro-silence she had mistaken for ignorance and that had been, the entire time, a hand held flat and patient on a frightened animal. She thought: I was never hiding. I was being kept. And the tangle of it, the shame and the gratitude and the wasted years and the unbearable size of being loved like that, all of it rose through her at once and came out, absurdly, as:</p>

<p>"You sent me to her on purpose. The dentist. Of all the dentists in Moscow."</p>

<p>"Your tooth was rotting," Sasha said reasonably. "So was the other thing."</p>

<p>And Anna turned over in her arms, in the dark, and found her face, and kissed her, and this time there was no vodka and no darkness and no mistake, no one being reached for through anyone else; it was aimed, and it landed, three years and two weeks and one permanent restoration late, on exactly the mouth it was meant for, and Sasha received it the way you receive something that has traveled a very long way to reach you: carefully, and completely, and without any questions at all.</p>

<p>Later, Anna slept. Sasha lay listening to her breathe, the slow even rhythm she could have found in a room of a hundred sleepers, and waited, out of three years of habit, for the dreaming to start, for the quickened intervals, for the lips to move around the soft unfinished syllables of a conversation happening somewhere she could not follow.</p>

<p>Tonight, the lips did not move at all.</p>

<p>Sasha listened for a long time to make sure. Then she closed her eyes, and let the habit go, one more piece of luggage set down on the platform, and slept.</p>

<p>Outside, the snow went on falling over Moscow, over the lit garlands and the empty morning streets, over a clinic gone dark on the other side of the river where a small calendar lay open to the last day of the year, unhurried, uncountable, and every flake of it, given time, came down through the dark and the streetlight gold and landed, at last, in its own place.</p>
