<p align="center"><em>Senza accompagnamento, rubato; poi attacca il finale</em></p>
<p align="center">***</p>

<p>The email arrived at nine in the evening, polite and patient and armed, the way deadlines are.</p>

<p>Three attachments. Three pieces of music, shortlisted by a choreographer who had been asking the same question for two weeks in progressively gentler fonts: the season is coming, the ice is booked, the federation wants a name for the free program by Friday, and all of that was administrative language for the one question underneath, which nobody in the building had dared to ask her out loud since February. Who are you going to be out there?</p>

<p>Sasha read it standing at the kitchen counter with a fork in one hand, and put the phone face-down, and finished eating, and washed the plate, and walked the dog around the block, and came back, and did every small honest thing a person can do to a Tuesday evening, and at half past eleven she was lying in the dark on her back with the ceiling above her and the phone on her chest, rising and falling, rising and falling, and she had answered nothing.</p>

<p>11:48.</p>

<p>She played the first track with one earbud in, lying down. It came at her the way she had come at everything her whole life, drums first, a wall of guitars with a door blown in it, music that took the room by the collar. It was magnificent. It was exactly what they wanted. She could see the whole program inside the first thirty seconds, the costume, the red, the fists coming up on the beat, eighteen thousand people getting what they had paid to see, the quad queen, the rocket, the girl who burns; she could see the campaign posters. She listened to a minute and forty of it and understood, with a precision that arrived like nausea, that she could skate this program in her sleep, because she had already skated it, in February, with her mind slamming doors the whole four minutes while her body told the truth behind her back.</p>

<p>A costume nailed shut around a person. That was what the world would take at full volume, and she knew now what it cost to wear it.</p>

<p>She turned onto her side. The phone slid to the mattress.</p>

<p>12:37.</p>

<p>The second track was the safe one, big and cinematic and belonging to nobody, strings out of a film about some other country's war, music that would carry any skater and reveal none. Halfway through it she realized she had stopped listening entirely and was lying with her eyes open thinking about a bench, and a book closed all the way, and a lead lying on a path with nobody picking it up, and she shut the track off with more force than a thumb requires.</p>

<p>She turned onto her other side. The streetlight came through the curtain gap and lay in a thin blade across the floor, and she watched it not move.</p>

<p>1:26.</p>

<p>The third track she did not play at all, because she already knew what it was, the choreographer had flagged it as the wild card, something slow, and she was not, at half past one in the morning, in a condition to be alone in the dark with something slow.</p>

<p>She got up. The dog raised her head from the basket in the corner and assessed the situation and declined to participate.</p>

<p>There was a version of the old exhibition costume somewhere under the bed, in one of the flat boxes, and it had a cut on the shoulder that might work for the new one if she could just look at it, and this was the reason she gave herself for kneeling on the floor of her own room at half past one in the morning and dragging out, one by one, the shallow cardboard strata of an athlete's life. Medals in a tin that no longer closed. Costumes in tissue paper gone soft as cloth. Programs, banquet lanyards, a folder of contracts, the sediment of eleven years, and at the back, against the wall, where a thing ends up when it has been pushed a little further from the light every year without ever once being moved on purpose, a shoebox with nothing written on it.</p>

<p>She knew what it was before her hand touched it. Her hand touched it anyway.</p>

<p><em>Rubato.</em></p>

<p>She sat on the floor with her back against the bed and the box on her knees, in the streetlight, in the flat's small nighttime weather of radiator tick and refrigerator hum, and she did not open it for a long time, the way you stand in the cold outside a door you have every right to walk through.</p>

<p>Then she opened it.</p>

<p>A screw. That was the first thing her fingers found, small and cold and rolled into the corner seam of the box, one single blade screw of the sort that lives in every skate bag in the world, except that this one had never lived in a skate bag; it had lived here, for years, because a girl of thirteen had been handed two on a corridor floor and told, by somebody who was always right about practical things, you'll lose one on the way home. She had not lost it. She had used the first and kept the second, and the keeping had never once been examined, the way you do not examine a floorboard you have simply always stepped over, and she sat now with eleven years of it in her palm, a screw that had outlasted four pairs of boots, two Olympic cycles, one federation, and every single public version of herself, because at thirteen, without knowing she was doing it, she had decided that a thing that girl gave her was not for spending.</p>

<p>Under the tissue there was a bus ticket. Kazan, or the road near it, the ink gone the color of weak tea, the date still legible if she tilted it to the light, and she did not need to tilt it to the light. The seat number was 9. She had been in 11, hanging over the back of it, demanding to know what happened in a book where nothing happened. Four hundred pages of a man deciding whether to visit his mother, read aloud at a volume pitched for one listener, and she had kept the ticket the way drunks keep corks, not to remember the wine but to prove to some future disbelieving self that the evening had existed.</p>

<p>She laid the ticket on the floor beside the screw, squarely, the way you lay out evidence.</p>

<p>The box held other strata. A wristband from a junior event in a country that no longer invited them. A pin. A folded start order with two names on it separated by one line of type and no fence post at all, from the years before the fence post was invented. She took each thing out and turned it over and set it down in the row on the floor, and the row grew, and somewhere in the middle of the growing she stopped being able to pretend to herself that she was looking for a costume, and the flat went on ticking and humming around a girl sitting on the floor of her own room at two in the morning conducting an inventory she had refused for eleven years.</p>

<p>2:19, said the phone, face-up on the carpet, uninvited.</p>

<p>Everything in the box was Anna. That was the finding of the inventory, and the finding was not that the box existed, every athlete has a box, but that the box had been *curated*: eleven years, hundreds of banquets, dozens of teammates, a whole loud crowded gorgeous life, and the shoebox at the back of it all contained one girl, sorted by no principle except the only principle, and Sasha had done the sorting herself, item by item, year by year, each time telling herself nothing at all, filing without a label, and there is a word for an archive that the archivist hides from the archivist, and the word had been standing in her chest since a March afternoon at the boards, patient as a held note, waiting for her to run out of other rooms.</p>

<p>She picked up the phone. Not to check the time.</p>

<p>The note was still there, where notes live, three autumns down the scroll. STEP, IT'S JUST A STEP. Her own capitals, her own lie, drafted in a hotel room in a city she had never bothered to learn, and under it the little diagram only she could read, the entry that was not an entry, the wrist with the wait built into it, the shape of a space exactly one person wide. Her body had skated it in front of the whole watching planet with her mind slamming doors, and the whole watching planet had risen to its feet, and not one soul among the eighteen thousand had known what they were applauding, and she had stood in the wreckage of her own record with her hand held out to nobody and told herself she was free.</p>

<p>She read the note twice. Then she looked at the row on the floor, the screw, the ticket, the wristband, the pin, the start order, the small complete museum of a thing she had called a schedule, out loud, in the rain, to its face.</p>

<p>And she reached for the last item, and there was no last item.</p>

<p>That was the discovery waiting at the bottom of the box, underneath the tissue paper, in the empty corner: the collection was missing its founding piece, and it was missing it because the founding piece had never been an object. A Monday. A set of boards in Moscow. A girl of thirteen with one eyebrow a millimeter up, saying one sentence about a jump, and the sentence did not exist anywhere in the world, not in the box, not in a diary, not in a single retelling, because its keeper had understood by pure instinct at thirteen what she could not say to herself at twenty: that some things are not stored in boxes because boxes can be found, and had spent eleven years carrying it in the one archive nobody could open, and had told a girl on a bench in the rain that none of it had ever happened.</p>

<p>Sasha sat very still on the floor of her room.</p>

<p>The radiator ticked. The refrigerator changed its mind about something and settled. In the basket in the corner the dog sighed the sigh of the deeply put-upon, and outside a car went past through the wet street with a long soft tearing sound, and the girl on the floor put both hands over her face, not to cry, the crying was finished, but the way you cup your hands over a match, because something had lit and the room was full of drafts.</p>

<p>3:04.</p>

<p>She played the third track.</p>

<p>She played it lying on the floor, flat on her back among the evidence, both earbuds in, eyes on the ceiling, and it was slow, of course it was slow, it was the kind of music she had spent eleven years calling boring, no wall of drums, no door blown in, nothing coming at you that could be answered, nothing anywhere to jump to; a long line unspooling at its own unhurried temperature, asking for nothing, arriving anyway, the sound of somebody sitting on a bench turning pages while a whole winter argued with them and lost. It did not take the room by the collar. It took the room the other way, the way water takes a shore, by being there every single time the shore looked.</p>

<p>She saw the program before the track was half over. Not the posters, there would be no posters in it; she saw the ice, and the long entries, and the held edges her whole career had been too loud for, and she saw, at the far turn, where the music gathered itself and waited, the exact place where the step would go. The entry that was not an entry. The wrist with the space in it. Not buried this time, not smuggled past her own attention in a choreographic sequence at minute three; placed, in the open, at the center, where a step goes when the person who built it has stopped lying about the address.</p>

<p>The track ended. She lay in the silence after it with her heart doing the thing it had done in a final pose in February, going on and on, and this time she did not drive any post into any ground. She let it go on.</p>

<p>Then she got up off the floor, and there was nothing rubato about it.</p>

<p>The reply she typed standing in the kitchen in the dark took four lines and no drafts. The third one. Yes. Keep the ending long, don't cut the quiet section, I need room in the second half for a step I already have. She read it once and sent it at 3:51 in the morning, and the whooshing little sound it made leaving was the loudest thing the flat had produced all night.</p>

<p>She put the box back under the bed. The row of evidence she did not put back; she returned each piece to the tissue slowly, in order, except the screw, which she stood holding for a moment in the middle of her own room, and then did not return at all. It went into the inside pocket of her training jacket, the small zipped one over the chest that every skater has and none of them uses, where it made no weight and no sound and no sense, which put it in good company with everything else she had decided that night.</p>

<p>Some people carry spare elastics for other people. She had learned the currency late, but she had learned it.</p>

<p>The window was going gray at the edges. In four hours there would be ice, and a choreographer with a shortlist reduced to one, and a season with her name on it somewhere down the road, and further down the same road, though she did not know the date yet, a festival, a gala, a bright compulsory evening full of cameras where the world would ask its favorite question in its favorite way and she would answer it in the only language she had ever been fluent in.</p>

<p>She tied her hair back at the mirror in the gray light, and the elastic she used was black, and ordinary, and not hers, and it held.</p>

<p align="center"><em>Attacca.</em></p>
