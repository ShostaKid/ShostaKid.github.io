<p align="center"><em>Violin Concerto no. 2</em></p>
<p align="center"><em>I. Larghetto amoroso; poi un rubato non voluto</em></p>
<p align="center">***</p>

<p>The last of the seniors went at half past eight, and the coaches went at twenty to nine, and by nine o'clock the building had reached the hour when it stopped belonging to the federation and started belonging to whoever had not gone home.</p>

<p>Anna had not gone home. There was no reason for it that she could have written on a form. Her session had finished, her programme had been run twice and corrected once, her bag was packed and standing by the gate with the zip closed, and she was still on the ice at nine in the evening doing crossovers she did not need, in the enormous cooling quiet of a rink with nobody in it, listening to the sound her own blade made.</p>

<p>That was the honest reason, if anybody had asked. Nobody asked. In an empty building the ice reported everything, every edge, every change of weight, and a person could hear her own skating properly for once instead of hearing it through six other people and a speaker playing somebody's free programme too loud, and Anna Shcherbakova at fifteen would have given a great deal for one hour a day of hearing herself think.</p>

<p>The overhead lights went off in banks, on a timer, starting from the tunnel end. That was the building's way of asking you to leave. She had been here for the first bank at ten past nine, and the second at twenty past, and the ice had shrunk each time by a third, gold in the middle and blue at the edges, and she had gone on skating in the part that was left.</p>

<p>Behind her, from the tunnel, a sound like an engine turning over.</p>

<p>"You're still here."</p>

<p>"So are you."</p>

<p>"I live here." Sasha came down the steps with her guards already off and her jacket unzipped and her hair coming out of everything meant to be holding it. "I'm practically load-bearing. If I left, the roof would go."</p>

<p>She stepped onto the ice without stopping to think about it, which was how she did everything, and took the long way round the outside at a speed nobody uses at nine in the evening, and the sound of her arrived a half-second ahead of her the whole way: that scrape she had, aggressive, unapologetic, a blade that told the ice what was going to happen rather than asking.</p>

<p>Anna let her come. She kept her own line, unhurried, and did not look up, and the two sounds ran together for a moment in the dark half of the building, the scrape and the quiet cut, and then Sasha swung wide and came alongside and matched her stroke for stroke, badly, on purpose.</p>

<p>"You're doing the boring thing."</p>

<p>"It's called an edge."</p>

<p>"It's called being fifteen and having no fun." Sasha crossed in front of her, backward, grinning, entirely too close. "Nine o'clock. Empty rink. All this ice. And you're out here doing homework."</p>

<p>"Some of us are working on something."</p>

<p>"Some of us are hiding from your mother's car."</p>

<p>"That too," Anna admitted, and Sasha laughed so loudly that the sound went up into the dark of the roof and came back off the far boards, and the third bank of lights went out.</p>

<p align="center">***</p>

<p>The game was Sasha's idea. The game was always Sasha's idea; the game had been Sasha's idea since they were nine years old in the novice group at Khrustalny, standing in a line waiting for the coach to finish shouting at somebody else, and it had no name and no rules that either of them had ever said out loud.</p>

<p>One of you skated. The other one had to be your shadow.</p>

<p>That was all of it. You went where the leader went at the distance the leader set, left where she went right, matching from the hips down like a figure folded out of one piece of paper, and if you broke the shadow you lost. Nine-year-olds had invented it to torment each other in the ten minutes before ice time. It had survived six years, two coaches, one growth spurt each, and a federation reorganisation, and it survived because it was the only competition either of them had ever been in that had no marks attached to it.</p>

<p>"Come on," Sasha said. "One round. You're already warm and I'm bored and there's nobody here to tell us not to."</p>

<p>"You'll lose."</p>

<p>"I never lose."</p>

<p>"You lose constantly. You lost in March."</p>

<p>"March was a technicality and you know it," said the taller girl with enormous dignity, and pushed off backward, hands out. "Lead. Go on. Do your worst, Shcherbakova, bore me to death."</p>

<p>So Anna led.</p>

<p><em>Larghetto.</em></p>

<p>She started slow, because slow was where Sasha broke first, and because she liked the sound of it in an empty building: long edges, held out past where a lung would ordinarily ask for something, the blade laying a single unbroken line into ice that had not been touched since the resurfacer. One and. Two and. The vents breathing somewhere up in the roof. And behind her and half a beat off her left hip came the other sound, the loud one, dragged down to a speed it had never in its life wanted to travel at, matching her.</p>

<p>Anna held the edge longer. Sasha held it. Anna took it deeper, into the part of the curve where your own weight starts filing complaints, and Sasha followed her down into it and made a small noise of protest that was not, technically, a break.</p>

<p>"You're doing this on purpose."</p>

<p>"That's the game."</p>

<p>"This isn't skating, this is a nap with knives on."</p>

<p>Anna smiled at the ice where nobody could see it and turned into the next pattern.</p>

<p>They went round like that for a while, at a speed for which no scoreboard has ever awarded a single point, and Anna kept waiting for the break and it did not come, and somewhere in the fourth or fifth pattern she understood that Sasha was not going to break, that Sasha had decided to out-patient her out of pure spite and would keep it up all night, and something in Anna's chest went sideways in a way she had no name for and did not investigate.</p>

<p>"My turn," Sasha said.</p>

<p><em>Poco a poco accelerando.</em></p>

<p>And the tempo went out from under Anna like a rug.</p>

<p>She had known it was coming and it did not help. Sasha did not build; Sasha simply arrived at full speed the way weather arrives, three crossovers and then the long axis of the rink eaten in four strokes, and Anna went after her with her heart already climbing, because a shadow is not permitted to be late.</p>

<p>Left where she went right. In where she went out. The sound stopped reporting two skaters and started reporting a kind of sleet, edges flickering over faster than the ear could file, and the ice ran out ahead of them, and the second corner came in too fast, and Anna took it because there was no version of this in which she did not take it.</p>

<p>Sasha threw a turn in that had no business in the middle of a pattern. Anna answered it.</p>

<p>Sasha put a hop in on the exit, ridiculous, showing off, a thing she had invented three seconds ago. Anna answered that too, and heard Sasha laugh somewhere in front of her, delighted, and answered *that* by taking the next entry a half-metre tighter than she had been given, which was cheating, which was the whole point.</p>

<p>They went the length of the building like that with the lights down to two banks and the boards ticking with the cold and Anna's own breath going in and out of her at a rate she had entirely stopped supervising, and she was smiling, she discovered, she had been smiling for some time.</p>

<p><em>Come prima.</em></p>

<p>Sasha slowed. Sasha stopped, at the far end, in the gold, and turned around with her hands on her knees, breathing hard, hair completely destroyed.</p>

<p>"Okay," she said. "Okay. New rules."</p>

<p>"There are no rules."</p>

<p>"There's one now. Stakes." She straightened up. "Loser owes the winner a day."</p>

<p>Anna, gliding to a stop four metres away, said, "A day."</p>

<p>"A whole day. Winner's rules, winner picks everything, the loser does what she's told and doesn't complain and doesn't ask where we're going." Sasha's chin came up in the way it did when she had decided something and was daring the world to have an opinion. "No skating in it. Nothing useful in it. And the loser can't get out of it by being busy, because you're always busy, that's a thing you do."</p>

<p>The building hummed. Somewhere far off, a door.</p>

<p>And Anna Shcherbakova, aged fifteen, standing on an empty sheet of ice at twenty to ten at night, discovered that she did not know what she wanted.</p>

<p>This had not happened before. She had a very orderly interior; she had built it herself, over years, the way you build anything, and everything in it was in a place she could find in the dark. What she wanted was always available for inspection. And she stood there with her hands at her sides and put the question to herself in the plainest terms she had, *do you want to win this*, and the answer came back in two voices at once, both of them hers, and they were saying opposite things at the same volume.</p>

<p>Because she was going to win. That was simply arithmetic. Sasha had never in her life out-lasted her at slow and Anna had one clean patient pattern left in her legs that would end this in ninety seconds. And then there would be no day. There would be tomorrow's session at seven and the day after that one, and the girl at the far end of the ice would go on being the loudest thing in every room Anna walked into, and nothing would change, and nothing had to change, and she had never once asked for anything different.</p>

<p>And if she lost.</p>

<p>She did not finish the thought. She got about four words into it and stopped, because on the other side of those four words was something she had no word for at all, and Anna did not go into rooms without knowing what was in them.</p>

<p>"Well?" Sasha said.</p>

<p>"Fine."</p>

<p>"Say it properly."</p>

<p>"Loser owes a day," Anna said. "Lead."</p>

<p><em>Rubato.</em></p>

<p>Sasha led, and it was fast from the first stroke, and Anna went after her.</p>

<p>She was fine for the first pattern. She was fine into the second. And somewhere in the middle of the second, on a long inside edge with the boards coming up, with her body doing what it had been trained for nine years to do without her, the question arrived again, uninvited, at the worst possible moment, in the small unattended part of her mind that ought to have been counting.</p>

<p>*And if you lost.*</p>

<p>It was not even a decision. That was the thing she would go over afterward and be unable to explain to herself: she did not decide anything, she did not slow down, she did not choose. There was simply one beat, one single beat somewhere between the entry and the exit, in which Anna Shcherbakova was not entirely present, and a shadow cannot afford a beat.</p>

<p>Sasha turned out of the pattern.</p>

<p>Anna turned a half-beat late, on the wrong edge, and had to put a foot down.</p>

<p>The sound of it was very small. One scrape, a stop that was not in anybody's programme, in a building with two banks of lights on.</p>

<p>And then a noise came out of the far end of that rink that would have been excessive at a European Championship, and Sasha Trusova came back down the ice at a speed the maintenance staff would not have approved of, both fists in the air, shouting something with no words in it at all, and hit the boards beside Anna with her whole body and slid down them and had to be helped up, still shouting.</p>

<p>"Say it. Say it out loud, I want to hear it."</p>

<p>"You won."</p>

<p>"Louder."</p>

<p>"You won, Sasha."</p>

<p>"I WON." She wheeled away with her arms out and came back. "I won, I have a day, I have an entire day of Anna Shcherbakova and she cannot complain about any of it, tomorrow, you're not busy tomorrow, I checked with your mother on Tuesday."</p>

<p>Anna's eyebrows went up. "You checked on Tuesday."</p>

<p>"Not relevant." Sasha was already skating backward toward the gate, pointing at her with both hands. "Nine o'clock. Outside your building. Wear something you can move in and don't bring your own plan, I know you, you'll bring a plan, I'll find it and I'll confiscate it."</p>

<p>"What are we doing?"</p>

<p>"Absolutely not." She hit the gate, went up the steps two at a time in her guards, and turned in the tunnel mouth with the last of the light on her. "Nine o'clock, Anya."</p>

<p>And she was gone, and the noise of her went off down the corridor and through two doors and out of the building, and the rink was quiet again.</p>

<p align="center">***</p>

<p>Anna did not go and get her bag.</p>

<p>She stood where she was for a while, and then she skated slowly back down the ice and stopped on the patch where it had happened, which was easy to find, because the mark was still there: one short ugly scrape across an otherwise clean sheet, the width of a boot, in the place where a girl who did not miss had missed.</p>

<p>She looked at it.</p>

<p>Under her ribs something was going at a speed that had nothing to do with the last forty minutes of skating, and it had been going since the words *loser owes a day*, and it was still going now, in the quiet, with nobody chasing anybody, and Anna put her hand flat against her own sternum as though the pressure might be a form of instruction.</p>

<p>It did not slow down.</p>

<p>The last bank of lights went off at ten, and left her standing there in the dark with the cold coming up off the ice and the whole rest of her orderly interior perfectly in its place, everything findable, everything exactly where she had put it, and one room in it she had not opened, and a day tomorrow with no plan in it at all.</p>
