<p align="center"><em>II. Adagio religioso</em></p>
<p align="center">***</p>

<p>The curtains had been shut for nineteen days, and the room had adjusted to it, the way a room will, and had begun keeping its own weather.</p>

<p>There was a gray in there that had nothing to do with the hour. It lay on the desk and on the chair with the clothes on it and on the small pile of soft parcels by the door that somebody kept adding to and nobody carried in, and it did not change much between morning and evening, so that the only reliable way to tell time was the sound of the building: the lift at seven, the boy upstairs practising scales at four, the neighbor's television through the wall at nine with its laughter arriving on a delay, as though the joke had to travel.</p>

<p>Her mother came in twice a day and opened the window for eleven minutes exactly, and did not touch the curtains, because they had come to an agreement about the curtains without ever once discussing it.</p>

<p>Kamila slept a great deal and woke tired, which she understood to be an arrangement her body had made without consulting her. She ate what was put in front of her, some of it. She watched the ceiling. And out of a habit she could not break she would put two fingers to the side of her own throat and find it there and count.</p>

<p>It had gone slow. Not dangerous, only unfamiliar, a small motor idling in an empty garage because somebody had left it running, and she would lie in the gray and count down to fifty and lose interest in the number, and take her hand away, and go back to the ceiling.</p>

<p>The phone lay face-down on the desk and had lain there since the fourth day.</p>

<p>Alena's messages arrived anyway, at a rate of about two a week, which was a rate somebody had clearly thought about. <em>Kamilochka. Just saying hello.</em> Four days later, a photograph of a coffee with a badly drawn cat in the foam and no text at all. Then, <em>Nobody expects anything, I am legally required to inform you.</em> Then a long one, at one in the morning, about a boot fitting that had gone wrong and a fight with an equipment manager and a boy from the ice dance group who had developed opinions about her free leg, four hundred words of magnificent nothing, ending: <em>anyway. No reply needed. That's not a joke, I mean it, don't reply.</em></p>

<p>Kamila read all of them by tilting the phone up on the desk with one finger so the screen lit, and let it fall face-down again, and answered none.</p>

<p>Sasha did not write. That was its own kind of information, and Kamila, who was fifteen and no longer a fool about anything, read it correctly on the first day and did not hold it against her for a single hour.</p>

<p align="center">***</p>

<p>The envelope came on the twentieth day.</p>

<p>Her mother brought it in with the afternoon glass of water and set it on the desk and went out again without saying one word about it, which was the most tactful thing anyone did that entire spring.</p>

<p>It was ordinary and white and had her name on it in ink. Not a card with a printed sunrise. Not another parcel with something soft inside. An envelope with handwriting on the front of it, small and upright and evenly spaced, the writing of somebody who had been taught penmanship by a person who cared and had never afterward been in enough of a hurry to abandon it.</p>

<p>Kamila looked at it from the bed.</p>

<p>She knew what would be inside. She had three weeks of evidence: the ones from the federation, the ones from strangers, the ones from women who had known her mother in Kazan, and every one of them said the same three things in a different order, that she was strong, that this would pass, and that they were thinking of her, and every one of them required her to be a girl who was getting better, and reading them was a job of work she had stopped doing on the ninth day.</p>

<p>The envelope sat on the desk through the afternoon. It sat there through the neighbor's television and through her mother's second eleven minutes of window and through the long stretch of dark when the boy upstairs had finished his scales, and at some point that night Kamila got up, and crossed the room, and picked it up, and dropped it in the bin under the desk.</p>

<p>Then she got back into bed and lay with her eyes open for two hours.</p>

<p>At half past one she got up again and took it out.</p>

<p>It had gone down flat under a tissue and picked up a bend across one corner, and she stood in the dark in her socks and smoothed it against her thigh, twice, before she could stop herself doing it. The paper made a small sound. She sat on the edge of the bed with her back to the curtains and worked one finger under the flap and tore it badly, the way she did everything with her hands when she was in a hurry, and unfolded one sheet.</p>

<p><em>The bakery on the corner of Sadovaya has changed hands. The new man is a disaster. His bread is worse in every measurable way, he burns the bottoms, and he is the happiest person on that street. He sings while he does it. The woman who had it before him never sang once in eleven years and her bread was perfect. I have bought bread there four times this week trying to understand this and I am no closer.</em></p>

<p><em>The snow has gone off the pavements and stayed on the roofs. From the third floor the city looks like it is still winter, and from the street it does not, so there are two cities stacked on top of each other and everybody is walking around in the bottom one.</em></p>

<p><em>A.</em></p>

<p>Kamila turned it over. There was nothing on the back.</p>

<p>She read it again, both sides again, held it closer to the light from the gap under the door, and found nothing else there either: no question, no wish, no strength, no passing, no thinking of you. Two paragraphs about bread and roofs, from a person who had crossed a city to put them in a postbox.</p>

<p>She put it on the desk. She lay down. She got up eleven minutes later and put it in the drawer instead, because the desk was where things sat and the drawer was where things were kept, and she was so annoyed about having made that distinction that she pushed the drawer shut with her hip hard enough to rattle the handle.</p>

<p align="center">***</p>

<p>The second came two days later.</p>

<p>She opened it standing up, at the desk, still holding the water glass in her other hand, and afterward could not account for that at all.</p>

<p><em>There is a dog outside the pharmacy on Bolshaya every afternoon at ten past four. Not a stray. Well fed, collar, extremely calm. He arrives at ten past four and sits down and waits, and eventually somebody comes out and he leaves with them, and I have now watched this four times and it has been a different person twice. I do not know what arrangement this dog has made, or with whom, or on what terms. He appears satisfied with it.</em></p>

<p><em>The man in the flat below ours has been arguing with the parking machine on the corner. Eleven minutes on Monday, nine on Tuesday, and this afternoon he has brought out a chair.</em></p>

<p><em>A.</em></p>

<p>Kamila made a sound at the second paragraph, one short scrape of breath through the nose, over before she knew it was coming, and stood there afterward with the glass in her hand feeling as though she had been caught at something.</p>

<p>The third came two days after that, and she was awake for the post.</p>

<p>She did not admit this to herself in those words. What she did was wake at the hour her mother went down to the boxes, and lie listening to the lift, and count the seconds it took to come back up, and be lying flat with her eyes closed by the time the door opened.</p>

<p>On the day one did not come, she got out of bed at four in the afternoon for the first time in a week and a half, and stood in the middle of the room with her arms folded, furious with the postal service of the Russian Federation, and then furious with herself, and then simply standing in the middle of a gray room at four in the afternoon in her socks with nothing to do about any of it.</p>

<p>It came the next morning. She did not go back to bed after reading it.</p>

<p align="center">***</p>

<p><em>The ice went off the pond in the park in one night. Tuesday it was gray all over and Wednesday it was black and open, with four birds sitting on the edge of it looking personally deceived. I would have said it takes a week. It took a night.</em></p>

<p><em>Two women on the bench by the gate are conducting a feud. I have been watching it for a month. They arrive separately, sit at opposite ends of the same bench, do not speak, and neither of them will move to another bench, and both of them come early to be sure of their end. I do not know what happened between them. I hope it was enormous.</em></p>

<p><em>A.</em></p>

<p>She began answering them in her head, which she recognized at once as a bad sign and could not stop.</p>

<p>The baker sang because he was new to it, obviously, and did not yet understand that bread was a serious business, and in a year he would stop singing and the bread would be better, and whether that was a fair trade was exactly the sort of question the girl with the chestnut hair would go on buying four bad loaves a week to answer. Kamila lay in the gray composing this at some length, with corrections, and then lay there afterward with her forearm over her eyes, because there was nowhere to put it.</p>

<p>Twice she got as far as paper. The first time she wrote <em>Anya,</em> and looked at it, and could not find the next word, because the next word had to be about her and everything about her was the thing nobody in these letters had mentioned once. She tore the sheet into strips. The second time she got three lines in and read them back and heard how they sounded, a girl in a room explaining herself, and did that to those as well.</p>

<p>On the twenty-eighth day she crossed the room and put two fingers into the curtains and opened them eleven centimeters.</p>

<p>The light came in and landed across the desk in a bar so bright it looked solid, and she flinched from it, actually flinched, and put her hand up, and then stood at the gap with her eyes streaming and looked down at the street to see whether it was doing what the letters said it was doing.</p>

<p>It was. There was a man on the pavement with a rolled carpet on his shoulder, taking up the whole width of it. Two schoolgirls walking backward in front of a third, shrieking. A woman on a balcony opposite, shaking out a rug. The particular color the buildings went at that hour, which she had not thought about once since January and which arrived now with the whole of last spring folded inside it, the walk to the rink, the smell of thawing pavement, Sasha bellowing something from twenty meters back.</p>

<p>Kamila stood in the gap for a long time with her hand flat on the cold glass.</p>

<p>She closed the curtains again. She left the gap.</p>

<p align="center">***</p>

<p>She watched the footage on the thirty-first day, at two in the morning, which is when people do that.</p>

<p>Not the parts with herself in them. She had lived those and there was nothing in them she needed a second view of, and she went past them at speed with her thumb on the bar and her jaw set.</p>

<p>It was at the end. Eleven seconds, in the last minute of the broadcast, in a room she had walked past that night without going into: the room where the winner waits. And the winner was sitting in it by herself on a plastic chair with a soft toy in her lap and both hands folded on top of it, in a room built to hold a celebration, with the noise of everyone else's catastrophe coming through the wall.</p>

<p>She was not crying. She was not doing anything at all. She was sitting with her back straight in the particular way she sat when she was managing something, and her chin was up, and her eyes were fixed on the middle distance, and the camera did not stay with her, because the camera had better material elsewhere that night, and it cut away after eleven seconds and did not come back.</p>

<p>Kamila watched it four times.</p>

<p>She watched it a fifth time with the sound off, and on the fifth time she saw the hands: how they were folded on top of the toy, and how the thumb of the upper one moved once, twice, on the fabric, small, out of sight of anyone in the room, the way a person does a thing with their hands when there is nobody at all to hold onto.</p>

<p>She got up and found paper.</p>

<p>She wrote for an hour and a half, badly, her handwriting going to pieces in the second half the way it always did when she was in a hurry, three sheets and then a fourth. She wrote about the eleven seconds and the plastic chair. She wrote about the thumb. She wrote about a January evening two years ago with one sock on and eleven minutes on a cold bench, which she had never told anybody about, not Alena, not her mother, not the girl it was about. She wrote that everybody in the world had watched three girls that night and had only found two of them interesting. She wrote that the loneliest person in that building had been the one who won, and that nobody had said so, not once, not in any language, in seven weeks. She wrote that she was sorry it had taken a broadcast at two in the morning for her to see it. She wrote, at the bottom of the fourth sheet, with her hand shaking: <em>and if the thing you would not teach me in the corridor is still anywhere in the world, I want it now. I don't care what it costs. I have nothing to spend it on anyway.</em></p>

<p>She read it back at four.</p>

<p>Then she folded it in four, and squared the edges, and put it in the drawer of the desk on top of the letters, and closed the drawer, and did not send it.</p>

<p align="center">***</p>

<p>She turned the phone face-up on the thirty-fifth day.</p>

<p>To Alena: <em>The boy from ice dance is right about your free leg. Sorry.</em></p>

<p>The reply came inside a minute and ran to eleven consecutive messages, of which the first three were noise, the fourth was <em>I KNEW IT</em>, the seventh was a photograph of the same coffee cat, and the last was <em>ok. ok. good. that's enough. putting the phone down before I ruin it.</em> Kamila laughed at the ceiling, out loud, alone, in a gray room, and the sound was so strange in there that she stopped almost at once and lay very still, listening to the room not having it in it any more.</p>

<p>To Sasha it took her twenty minutes to write four words. <em>Sashka. Are you all right?</em></p>

<p>The dots came, and stopped, and came again, and the answer took four minutes to arrive and was eight words long.</p>

<p><em>I can't say I'm all right. But better. A little.</em></p>

<p>Kamila sat with the phone in both hands.</p>

<p>The dots came back and stayed a long time, much longer than what finally arrived could account for.</p>

<p><em>I hope you're all right, Kamilochka. Anna worries about you. She doesn't say so, obviously. She just goes quiet, and then I have to guess which of the fourteen things she's being quiet about it is, and lately it's always been you, so.</em></p>

<p>And then, forty seconds later, the way somebody says the real thing in a doorway with their coat already on:</p>

<p><em>She's been carrying it by herself since February and she won't put it down for me. She never has, not once in eleven years. So if she asks you for anything, Kamilochka, anything at all, say yes.</em></p>

<p>Kamila read it three times. Then she put the phone on the desk, face up, and left it that way.</p>

<p align="center">***</p>

<p>The letter came on a Thursday.</p>

<p><em>The two women on the bench have made it up. I do not know how. On Monday they were at opposite ends and today they were in the middle, sharing a bag of seeds and abusing the pigeons together, and I did not see it happen, and I am unreasonably upset about missing it. I had four theories. Now I will never know which of them was right.</em></p>

<p><em>The lilac at the end of Sadovaya has come out. It is far too early and it is doing it very badly and the weather will certainly punish it, and it does not appear to care in the slightest.</em></p>

<p>And then, under a gap, in the same small even hand:</p>

<p><em>There is a thing a girl of fourteen once asked me to teach her, in a corridor, with one sock on, and I refused, because I was seventeen and thought that refusing was the same as being right.</em></p>

<p><em>That girl has probably stopped wanting it by now. But I would like to teach it to her once, all the same.</em></p>

<p><em>A.</em></p>

<p>Kamila sat down on the floor with her back against the bed and the paper in both hands and read the last four lines eleven times, and outside the gap in the curtains the light moved off the desk and onto the wall, and under her ribs, for the first time since February, something began to go quickly.</p>

<p><em>Attacca.</em></p>
