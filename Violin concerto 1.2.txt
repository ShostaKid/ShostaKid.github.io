<p align="center"><em>Allegro con malizia</em></p>
<p align="center">***</p>

<p>Beijing had been built to hold the whole world at once, and it held it, the flags and the buses and the volunteers in their bright coats, the dining hall where forty countries ate at the same hour, the corridors wide enough for a delegation to pass a delegation, and inside all that architecture two girls from the same rink in Moscow could not find fifty meters of hallway to walk down together.</p>

<p>It was nobody's decision. That was the part the taller of them kept running her tongue over, the way you keep finding a chipped tooth. No one had forbidden anything. There had been no argument, no door closed hard, no sentence either of them could have quoted afterward as the place it went wrong. There had only been schedules, which are more efficient than arguments: separate practice slots, separate media calls, separate rooms along the same corridor with the same carpet running past all of them, and the slow administrative fact that a person you do not see is a person you eventually stop knowing how to see.</p>

<p>The questions began the day they landed.</p>

<p>"How does it feel to compete against your teammate?"</p>

<p>She had answered honestly, the first time. She had said that the girl with the chestnut hair was the hardest worker at Khrustalny and always had been, that she had watched her fix a step nobody would ever notice was broken, alone, in an empty rink at three in the afternoon, and that if there was a person in the world who deserved to be standing here it was that one. She had meant every word, and she had felt, saying it, something loosen in her chest for the first time in a year.</p>

<p>It ran nowhere. Two lines in an agency wire, buried under the technical content of her practice session, gone by evening.</p>

<p>Three days later, tired, at the end of a session that had gone badly, she was asked the same question by a different microphone and heard herself say that she had not come eight thousand kilometers to be somebody's teammate.</p>

<p>That was everywhere within the hour. That was on the front of things, in three languages, with her name in bold and Anna's beside it and a slash between them like a fence post driven into the ground.</p>

<p>So she learned, and it took one afternoon, because she had always been a fast study when the lesson was physical and this turned out to be a physical lesson. Give them warmth and it evaporates. Give them a blade and it travels. The world had made its preferences perfectly clear, and Alexandra Trusova, who had spent her whole life being told to be less and had never once managed it, discovered that there was exactly one shape of herself the world would take at full volume, and it was the sharp one.</p>

<p>Then the short program came, and the triple Axel threw her down in front of everybody, and she got up off the ice of an Olympic arena in fourth place with nothing under her at all.</p>

<p>Fourth. Off the podium entirely, with one program left in her career at these Games and three girls ahead of her, two of them from her own building.</p>

<p>She did not cry about it, which surprised the people who had been waiting to film her crying about it. She went back to the village and lay on the bed with her boots still on and looked at the ceiling for two hours, and somewhere in the second hour a thing that had been loose in her for a year finished coming apart quietly, without any noise at all, the way ice gives at the middle of a lake rather than the edge.</p>

<p align="center">***</p>

<p>The tunnel, on the day, smelled of cold rubber and floor polish and other people's hairspray.</p>

<p>She was doing what she always did before ice, which was to move: small jumps in place, shoulders rolling, jacket half off one arm, the body already three minutes ahead of the schedule. The warm-up group had come off. Out beyond the mouth of the tunnel the arena was making the low continuous sound of eighteen thousand people who have paid to see something happen.</p>

<p>The girl half a head shorter than her came past on the way to the wall where the coaches stood.</p>

<p>She stopped. She looked up at Sasha for a second and a half, and then reached into her jacket pocket and held something out, and it was a spare hair elastic, black, ordinary, the kind that lives in every skater's pocket in the world.</p>

<p>"Your left side's coming loose," Anna said. "You'll feel it in the second half."</p>

<p>And Sasha looked at the elastic lying in that steady, unhurried hand, and what rose in her was not gratitude. What rose in her was eleven minutes over fifty meters, and a tin of screws sorted by length, and a voice reading aloud in the dark of a bus somewhere outside Kazan, and all of it arrived at once and with a weight that had become, over two years of corridors, entirely unbearable, because you cannot be handed the beginning of a thing in the middle of its ending and be expected to say thank you.</p>

<p>"I'm fine," she said.</p>

<p>The hand stayed out a moment longer. Then it closed, and went back into the pocket, and the smaller girl nodded, once, courteously, the nod she gave people in corridors.</p>

<p>"Skate your own program," she said, and went to the wall.</p>

<p>Sasha stood in the tunnel with her heart going and turned the sentence over, and in the state she was in she could only find one side of it. Skate your own program. Don't chase me. Don't bother. I have already won and we both know the arithmetic and there is nothing left for you to do out there except be spectacular.</p>

<p>Fine, she thought.</p>

<p><em>Presto.</em></p>

<p>Then let me be spectacular.</p>

<p align="center">***</p>

<p>The ice in that arena was the best she had ever felt under a blade, hard and fast and true, and she took it at speed on the first crossover and knew inside four seconds that her body had come, that it was all there, every gram of the four years, and that whatever else this night was going to be it was not going to be careful.</p>

<p>Her name went up. The noise came down. She set her position, and the building held its breath, and the opening bars arrived, and the girl from Ryazan went to work.</p>

<p>The flip came first.</p>

<p>She hit the entry angry and left the ice with too much, which on any other night would have been a fall and tonight was simply a jump with room to spare in it, four rotations closed and opened and the blade set down clean on the far side of them, and the sound the building made was not applause yet, it was the sound eighteen thousand people make when they understand together that they are going to see something.</p>

<p>One.</p>

<p>She did not need a girl who sorted spare screws by length.</p>

<p>Her heart put a small foot wrong somewhere between two beats and recovered, and she was already into the transition and had no time to look at it.</p>

<p>The Salchow, thirty seconds later, out of a step she had done ten thousand times, and it was the cleanest thing she had ever done in competition, so clean that she felt the landing arrive under her before she had finished asking for it.</p>

<p>Two.</p>

<p>She did not need four hundred pages of a man deciding whether to visit his mother, read aloud in a third row in the dark, at a volume pitched for one listener.</p>

<p>The heart again. Not a flutter, nothing dramatic, only a beat arriving a fraction early and the one behind it arriving a fraction late to cover for it, the small dishonest correction of an accompanist who has lost the page and does not want the soloist to know.</p>

<p>She went into the toe loop with her jaw set.</p>

<p>She did not need to be the only person in the building who knew whose wrists had been thinner in March than in October. She did not need to have carried bread in her bag for two years for someone who would never ask for it. She did not need to have noticed. She had never needed to notice. She was setting it down now, here, on Olympic ice, and the world could have it, and she jumped.</p>

<p>And the ice came up half a beat early.</p>

<p>Her hand went down. It went down flat, hard, the whole palm slapping the surface as she fought the landing round and got it, got it upright, got it moving, four rotations credited and the exit ugly and her ears full of a noise the arena made that was not quite the noise it had made for the first two.</p>

<p>Three.</p>

<p>Fourteen years of that body, and it had never once put a hand down on a toe loop. Not at ten. Not with a stress fracture. Not in the season she trained through a hip that screamed on every landing.</p>

<p>She came out of the transition with her mouth in a hard line and something white and rising behind her eyes, because there is nothing on this earth more insulting than a body that agrees with the thing you are trying to kill.</p>

<p>This is what you wanted, she thought, and she was no longer sure who she was saying it to, the wire services or the thumbnails or the fence post between two names or the traitor under her own ribs. You wanted a rivalry. I gave you the truth first and you buried it on page nine, so here. Here is the girl you asked for.</p>

<p>You want to see Alexandra Trusova?</p>

<p><em>Fortissimo.</em></p>

<p>Then look.</p>

<p>She threw herself into the Lutz.</p>

<p>It was the biggest jump of her life and she knew it in the air, in that strange elongated quiet that comes when the body has done everything correctly and there is nothing left to do but wait for the ice; and then the ice came, and the landing was not there, and she took it anyway. She took it on an edge that had no business holding her, with her free leg fighting for the horizon and her whole side burning, and she dragged it upright out of pure refusal and got the exit and did not fall, because the thing she was made of does not fall on request, and the arena came off its seats.</p>

<p>Four.</p>

<p>She did not need one sentence, spoken at a set of boards in Moscow, by a girl of thirteen with one eyebrow a millimeter up, on a Monday, about a jump. She did not need to have kept it. She did not need to have never once repeated it to anybody, not to her mother, not to her closest friend at the rink, not to the diary she had never written, because saying it aloud would have used some of it up.</p>

<p>That one her heart did not cover for. It went wrong for three beats in a row, out of time, out of shape, a stumble the whole body felt through the ribs, and she carried it into the choreographic sequence with her teeth together.</p>

<p>And the choreographic sequence was where the step lived.</p>

<p>She had put it there in the autumn, in a hotel room in a city she could not now name, and had told herself in the note on her phone exactly what it was, which was nothing, which was a step, which was just a step. Her body arrived at it the way a body arrives at anything it has done four hundred times, without consulting her. The entry that was not quite an entry. The wrist, turning, with the hesitation built into it, the small delay she had shaped so that a second skater could have come through the space at that exact moment, if there had been a second skater, if this had been a program for two, if the world had been arranged some other way.</p>

<p>Her mind said no.</p>

<p>Her mind said no with everything it had, in the voice she had used for a year on every microphone and in every corridor she had walked down alone, said it the way you slam a door you have already slammed so many times that the frame no longer fits it.</p>

<p>Her body skated it anyway. Perfectly. Better than she had ever skated it, better than the autumn, better than any of the four hundred, and the building came apart at that step, and not one of the eighteen thousand, not the six languages, not the coaches at the wall, not the girl standing in the tunnel with a spare elastic in her jacket pocket, knew what they were applauding.</p>

<p>She did.</p>

<p>She went into the last jump with her vision going white at the edges and her legs full of glass and nothing left in the tank except the thing that had put her on ice at four years old and kept her there through every injury and every headline, and she threw it, the fifth, into a body that had no business giving it to her, and the body gave it, and the combination held.</p>

<p>Five.</p>

<p>And there was nothing left to set down. She reached for the next thing to throw away and found the drawer open and the drawer empty, all of it lying out on the ice behind her in a long bright wreckage, sorted screws and bus windows and bread and one sentence about a Monday, and the music was ending, and she was still going, and the arena was standing up in sections like weather crossing a field.</p>

<p align="center">***</p>

<p>The last beat came and she hit the final position.</p>

<p><em>Fermata.</em></p>

<p>Eighteen thousand people made a noise she would never afterward be able to remember. A flag went up somewhere. A coach turned to another coach. The ice under her was scarred everywhere with what she had done to it, five white gouges in a surface that had been perfect four minutes ago, and the quad queen of Russia stood in the middle of the wreckage with her chest heaving and her chin up and thought, deliberately, with everything she had, the way you drive a post into ground:</p>

<p>I am Alexandra Trusova.</p>

<p>That is who I am. That is all I am. That is what I came here to be.</p>

<p>Her right hand was still up in the final position, and it had arrived there by itself the way the whole program had arrived by itself, and the wrist was turned, and there was a small delay in it, a hesitation exactly the width of a person who was not there.</p>

<p>And her eyes, without being sent, went to the tunnel.</p>

<p>The girl half a head shorter than her was standing in its mouth with her jacket zipped to the throat and her hands at her sides, waiting to be called, watching the ice she was about to be given. She did not wave. She would not have waved; that was not a thing she did. She simply stood there in the dark of the tunnel being the reason for every single thing that had just happened out here, and being the one person in the building who had not asked to be, and Sasha, who had spent four minutes and every gram of herself proving to the entire world that she was free, looked straight at her and understood, in the space of one held breath, that she had not moved so much as a centimeter.</p>

<p>Under her ribs, at a speed that had nothing to do with four minutes of skating, something went on and on and would not slow down.</p>

<p><em>Ma non troppo.</em></p>
