<p align="center"><em>Presto con amore; sempre stringendo</em></p>
<p align="center">***</p>

<p>Sasha slept about two hours and did not mind at all.</p>

<p>She had won at twenty to ten. By half past four she was awake again, flat on her back in the dark, running the whole thing from the beginning the way she ran a programme the night before a competition. The order of it. The timings. Where the money went, and what came after what.</p>

<p>Except that a programme lasted four minutes. This was eleven hours. A programme had a scoreboard at the end of it, and this had nothing at the end of it whatsoever, and she still could not lie still.</p>

<p>She had been building it since Tuesday. That part she was not going to mention to anybody. On Tuesday she had asked Anna's mother, very casually, whether Saturday was clear, four whole days before there was any bet to win. Four days of assembling an itinerary for a day she had no right to, on the pure assumption that she would find some way to get her hands on it.</p>

<p>She did not examine that either. Sasha's interior was not a filing system. It was a room with everything on the floor, and she found what she needed by kicking through it, and she had never in her life felt any urge to label a single item in there. All she knew was this: for about two years now, in any room, at any rink, at any banquet, in any minibus, some part of her had always known exactly where Anna Shcherbakova was standing. Not on purpose. The way you always know where the door is.</p>

<p>And last night the girl who never missed had missed. Now there was a Saturday.</p>

<p>Her chest had been going since four o'clock. She put it down to the two hours of sleep.</p>

<p><em>Presto.</em></p>

<p align="center">***</p>

<p>At two minutes to nine she was leaning on the wall outside Anna's building. At nine exactly the door opened, because of course it did.</p>

<p>Sasha turned to say something devastating and got out perhaps one syllable of it.</p>

<p>"What," said Anna.</p>

<p>"Nothing. Nothing at all."</p>

<p>"You made a noise, Sasha. I heard it from the steps."</p>

<p>"That wasn't a noise, that was a medical event." Sasha had both hands over her mouth and was failing completely. "Anya. Anneshka. Turn around for me. No, properly, all the way round, I want the full three-sixty, I may never see this again as long as I live."</p>

<p>Because the girl coming down the steps was wearing a navy tracksuit and a pair of trainers that had clearly done four winters of service. Her face was bare. Her hair hung over one shoulder in a single plain plait, the way it had when they were eleven, and she looked about fourteen years old, and she looked like somebody's extremely sensible daughter on the way to a maths olympiad. There was not one single item anywhere on her that said three-time national champion.</p>

<p>"You told me to wear something I could move in."</p>

<p>"I did say that."</p>

<p>"So I found something I could move in. I went through the entire wardrobe, Sasha, I gave it forty minutes." Anna arrived at the bottom of the steps and stopped there with her chin up, in the manner of a person who has followed an instruction to the letter and expects the appropriate marks for it. "Is it wrong? You said no plan, so I couldn't ask what we were doing, so I dressed for everything. If it's wrong, you have only yourself to blame, and I'd like that noted."</p>

<p>"It's noted. It's perfect. I need to sit down."</p>

<p>She sat down on the wall and laughed for some considerable time. Anna waited it out with her arms folded, entirely unmoved, and behind her the morning came up over the roofs and put a hard clean light on the wet road and the parked cars and that ridiculous plait.</p>

<p>Sasha wiped her eyes with the back of her wrist. Her chest did the thing again. She put it down to the sleep.</p>

<p>Then she noticed the bag.</p>

<p>"What's that?"</p>

<p>"A bag."</p>

<p>"Anya. I said no plan."</p>

<p>"A bag is not a plan. A bag is a container." Anna's grip on the strap tightened by a small but perfectly measurable amount. "There is a very great difference, and you know there is."</p>

<p>"Hand it over."</p>

<p>"Absolutely not."</p>

<p>"You lost a bet, Shcherbakova, and the terms were the loser does as she's told without complaining, and I distinctly heard you agree to them at twenty to ten last night in front of an empty rink." Sasha held out her hand. "Bag."</p>

<p>There was a short silence, in which two members of the national team conducted an entire negotiation without either of them saying a word. Then the bag changed hands.</p>

<p>Sasha unzipped it on the wall and began removing the contents one item at a time, laying them out along the top of the wall like evidence at a trial.</p>

<p>A litre of water. A second, smaller bottle of water. A packet of plasters. Hand sanitiser. A folding umbrella. Two protein bars. A charger, a spare charger, and a folded plastic rain poncho of the sort they give out free at stadiums.</p>

<p>"It's forecast clear all day."</p>

<p>"It was forecast clear in Sochi."</p>

<p>"That was three years ago!"</p>

<p>"And I was wet for six hours, and you laughed at me for four of them, and then you caught a cold and I gave you my scarf." Anna's eyebrow went up by its customary millimetre. "I keep records, Trusova. I have a very long memory and a great deal of free time in hotel rooms."</p>

<p>Sasha packed the umbrella away, zipped the bag shut, and slung it over her own shoulder, where it remained for the rest of the day.</p>

<p>"You'll get all of it back at nine tonight," she said. "Umbrella, ponchos, both chargers, the entire mobile hospital. Nine o'clock sharp, everything returned, no questions asked."</p>

<p>"And between now and then?"</p>

<p>"Between now and then you don't get to know what time it is either." Sasha turned her hand over, palm up, and waggled the fingers. "Watch."</p>

<p>Anna looked at her for a long moment.</p>

<p>"That was not in the terms."</p>

<p>"The terms are whatever I say they are. That's what winning means, Anneshka, and if you'd wanted a say in it you should have kept your feet on the second pattern." She kept the hand out. "Come on. It's one day. You've had fifteen years of knowing exactly what time it is, and frankly it shows."</p>

<p>To Sasha's genuine astonishment, after a pause of about two seconds, the girl in the tracksuit unfastened the watch from her left wrist and put it into her palm. It was warm from the skin. Anna's hand went straight into her pocket afterward, which was a thing Sasha had never once seen her do.</p>

<p>"Nine o'clock," Anna said.</p>

<p>"Nine o'clock," Sasha agreed, and put it away in her jacket.</p>

<p>She did not mention that in the eleven seconds it took them to cross the road, the fingers of Anna's other hand went twice to the bare place where the watch had been.</p>

<p align="center">***</p>

<p>The first stop was a kiosk on the corner of a street Anna had walked past several hundred times in her life without once stopping. There was a hand-painted sign above the window and a man behind it who had been turning the same vertical spit since before either of them was born.</p>

<p>"No."</p>

<p>"Yes."</p>

<p>"Sasha, I have a costume fitting on Monday morning, and Elena measures. She measures with a tape, and she writes the numbers in a book, and she reads the numbers from the last time out loud in front of everybody."</p>

<p>"Two shaurma, please," Sasha said to the window, "and don't listen to her, she's very famous and extremely rude about food."</p>

<p>What arrived was the size of a forearm, wrapped in paper, and hot enough to constitute a hazard. Anna received hers with both hands. She held it slightly away from her body and studied it the way you study a document somebody has asked you to sign without reading.</p>

<p>"What's in it?"</p>

<p>"Nobody knows."</p>

<p>"Sasha."</p>

<p>"Nobody knows, Anya, that's the beauty of the thing. Meat, definitely. Cabbage. The white sauce and the red sauce, and about a tablespoon of pure ambition." Sasha bit into her own and spoke around it. "And you have to eat it standing up. You can't sit down with a shaurma, it's disrespectful to the shaurma."</p>

<p>Anna took a bite. Her eyebrows climbed half a millimetre and stayed there. She took a second, considerably larger bite, at which point roughly a third of the white sauce escaped and went down the back of her hand.</p>

<p>She stood there on a Moscow pavement at twenty to ten in the morning, holding a forearm of hot meat in one hand, with sauce on her wrist and nowhere on earth to put it, and looked up at Sasha with an expression of such complete helplessness that Sasha had to turn away and say something to a lamppost.</p>

<p>"There's a napkin."</p>

<p>"Where?"</p>

<p>"In your bag."</p>

<p>"Which you have."</p>

<p>"Which I have," Sasha agreed, delighted. "Would you like a plaster as well? Some hand sanitiser? I've got the whole field hospital here, Shcherbakova, just say the word."</p>

<p>"I would like the napkin, and I would like you to stop enjoying yourself so obviously, and I would like both of those things in that order."</p>

<p>"Then you want the impossible, my little champion." Sasha handed the napkin over anyway, and watched her clean her wrist with the exact fastidiousness she brought to taping a boot.</p>

<p>Anna ate the whole thing. That was the part Sasha had not expected and would think about for a long time afterward. Not a polite third of it. All of it, standing up, on the street, and then she folded the paper twice, put it in the bin, and delivered her verdict in the tone of somebody submitting a report to a federation committee.</p>

<p>"That was the best thing I have eaten in four years."</p>

<p>"I know it was."</p>

<p>"It was better than the restaurant in Turin. It was better than that place in Osaka everybody made such a fuss about. It was three hundred roubles and it came out of a window, Sasha, and I would like it noted that I am not allowed to say any of this in an interview."</p>

<p>"You're not in an interview."</p>

<p>Something happened to Anna's face. Some part of the outer wall came down without anybody deciding to demolish it, and she looked away down the street with her hands in her pockets.</p>

<p>"No," she said. "I'm not, am I."</p>

<p align="center">***</p>

<p>The karting place sat under a flyover in the south of the city. It smelled of rubber and petrol and cold concrete, and the first race went precisely as Sasha had known it would.</p>

<p>Anna drove beautifully. That was the entire problem. She took the racing line the way she took a step sequence, correctly and in advance, decelerating into every corner along a smooth planned curve, and she was four full seconds a lap slower than everybody else in the building, including a twelve-year-old boy in a Spartak jacket. Sasha lapped her twice and hung out of the side of her kart to shout abuse on both occasions.</p>

<p>They pulled the helmets off in the pit lane. Anna's plait had come apart down one side. Her face was pink and she was breathing very deliberately through her nose.</p>

<p>"Something to say?"</p>

<p>"No."</p>

<p>"Nothing at all?"</p>

<p>"The line I was taking was correct," said Anna, to the timing screen.</p>

<p>"Oh, it was flawless. Genuinely. It was the most correct driving I have ever witnessed in my life, and the twelve-year-old is still laughing about it over there with his father."</p>

<p>"He cut the corner."</p>

<p>"Anneshka, he cut every corner. Everybody cuts every corner. That is what the corners are for." Sasha leaned back against the tyre wall, thoroughly at her ease. "Shall I tell you your problem? You brake before anything happens. You drive like the corner might have an opinion about you, and then you wait to hear what it is."</p>

<p>Anna looked at the track for a while.</p>

<p>Then she put the helmet back on, and said from inside it, in a voice with something entirely new sitting in the middle of it, "Again."</p>

<p><em>Stringendo.</em></p>

<p>She drove the second race like a woman committing a crime.</p>

<p>She took the first corner without lifting at all. The back end came round, and she caught it, badly, on nothing but nine years of instinct for things going wrong underneath her. After that she simply stopped decelerating for anything whatsoever. She went through the chicane on the wrong side. She put two wheels on the kerb at the hairpin and came off it sideways in a shower of dust. On the inside of turn four she hit Sasha, deliberately, with a small crunch and no apology of any kind, and went past.</p>

<p>Sasha spun gently to a halt in a cloud of her own tyre smoke and laughed until a marshal shouted at her.</p>

<p>Anna won by eleven seconds. She climbed out with her hands visibly shaking and dragged the helmet off, and her hair had abandoned the project entirely, and she stood in the pit lane staring at the timing screen with the most undignified expression Sasha had ever seen on her face.</p>

<p>"Again."</p>

<p>"We've got all day, Anya. There's no hurry."</p>

<p>"Sasha. Again."</p>

<p>They ran four more. She took three of them.</p>

<p align="center">***</p>

<p>After that the day went off the rails in exactly the direction Sasha had built it to go.</p>

<p>They crossed the city on the metro standing up, hanging off the same rail, conducting a loud argument about whether Anna's second race would have earned a penalty under actual regulations. Anna maintained that it would not have. By the third stop, most of the carriage had opinions.</p>

<p>They ate ice cream in a park in weather roughly six degrees too cold for ice cream. Sasha put her on a swing in a children's playground, a real one, with chains, and Anna approached it with tremendous seriousness, got herself frighteningly high, and came off it laughing with both hands over her face.</p>

<p>Sasha kept the watch in her jacket pocket and stopped counting the time. She had started counting something else instead.</p>

<p>Anna's sentences were getting longer.</p>

<p>At nine in the morning she had been running at three words a turn. By eleven she had worked up to whole clauses. And at some point in the middle of the afternoon, sitting on a low wall outside a shooting gallery with something unidentifiable on a stick, she said, with nobody having asked her anything at all:</p>

<p>"I don't know what I do on Saturdays."</p>

<p>"What?"</p>

<p>"When there isn't a competition and there isn't a session. I've been thinking about it since the karting, and I genuinely cannot tell you." She turned the stick over in her fingers. "I do the physiotherapy. I do the language homework, and then I do the extra language homework, because my tutor thinks I'm behind on the subjunctive. I go to bed at ten. That is the whole of it, Sasha. That has been the whole of it since I was eleven years old."</p>

<p>"Anya."</p>

<p>"And today I have eaten meat off a street and driven into you at a hairpin. I have been on a swing. I keep waiting for the part where I feel that I've wasted the day, because that is the part that always comes, and it hasn't arrived, and I have no idea what time it is." She looked up, entirely unguarded, with the sun in her eyes. "It might be four. It might be six. You've got my watch."</p>

<p>Sasha sat very still for a moment.</p>

<p>"Do you want it back?"</p>

<p>"No," said Anna.</p>

<p>"Say that again for me."</p>

<p>"You heard me perfectly well the first time."</p>

<p>"I did," Sasha said, "and I am going to be completely insufferable about it for the rest of my natural life, and you knew that when you opened your mouth."</p>

<p>"I did know it." Anna bit the last of whatever it was off the stick, with dignity. "I said it anyway. Where are you taking me next, Trusova?"</p>

<p align="center">***</p>

<p>Where she was taking her next was the top of a drop tower on the far side of the river, at twenty past seven in the evening, with the whole of Moscow coming on underneath them and Anna Shcherbakova's fingers welded to the safety bar.</p>

<p>"This is not a sport."</p>

<p>"Nobody said it was a sport."</p>

<p>"Sport has a purpose. Sport has a governing body and a scoring system, and this has a queue and a photograph at the end of it." The car clanked upward another metre. "Sasha. How high does it go."</p>

<p>"Higher."</p>

<p>"That is not a number."</p>

<p>"Anya, in about nine seconds you are going to be extremely famous inside your own head, and I want you to look at the river while it happens."</p>

<p>"I don't want to look at the river."</p>

<p>"Look at the river. It's the whole city. It's the best view in Mos,"</p>

<p><em>Precipitando.</em></p>

<p>The floor went out from under both of them.</p>

<p>What came out of Anna Shcherbakova on the way down was a sound Sasha had never heard in five years of knowing her. She would have paid money for a recording. It was not a scream. It was a short furious involuntary shout of outrage, one syllable long, and then a second one, and after that nothing at all but wind.</p>

<p>They came off the brakes at the bottom with their hair everywhere and their legs entirely gone.</p>

<p>They made it as far as a bench, four metres away, which was as far as either of them was getting.</p>

<p>Anna sat down and put her face in her hands and laughed. Not the small one. Not the one she gave to journalists, which came out level and finished on time. This one was completely undefended, and it went on and on, and it bent her double, and she put one hand flat on Sasha's knee for balance because there was nowhere else in the world to put it.</p>

<p>"You," she managed, when she had some air back, "are a lunatic. You are an actual lunatic, and I have known it since we were nine, and I got into the car anyway."</p>

<p>"I've been told."</p>

<p>"I have a fitting on Monday."</p>

<p>"Your fitting is fine. Your hair is a national emergency."</p>

<p>"My hair." Anna put a hand up to it, encountered the situation, abandoned the project immediately, and laughed again. Then she looked up.</p>

<p><em>Sempre presto.</em></p>

<p>Sasha's heart was going far too fast, and getting faster, which is the opposite of what a heart does four minutes after nine seconds of falling.</p>

<p>And sitting on that bench, she understood that she had been getting this wrong all day.</p>

<p>Since four o'clock in the morning. Since the wall outside the building, and the sauce on the wrist, and the helmet coming off in the pit lane. Since *do you want it back*, and *no*. None of it had been the sleep. None of it had been the ride. Every single time today that her chest had gone off like this, there had been one person standing in front of her, and there was one person in front of her now: pink-faced, wrecked, laughing, with her hair in ruins and a navy tracksuit on, at half past seven on a Saturday evening.</p>

<p>Oh, Sasha thought.</p>

<p>Her hand had already moved. It had gone out and taken hold of Anna's, the one still resting on her knee, and turned it over, and closed around it properly, palm to palm and fingers between fingers, before any part of Sasha had been consulted about whether she was going to do that.</p>

<p>Anna stopped laughing.</p>

<p>She did not take her hand back. She looked down at it, and then up, and the streetlight over the bench chose that moment to come on, and it was on her face, and she said nothing at all.</p>

<p>Under Sasha's fingers, at the base of the thumb where the wrist begins, something was going very fast indeed.</p>

<p>Fast, and getting faster.</p>

<p>Sasha looked at her.</p>

<p>And the girl who could read a warm-up from forty metres, who had known what Sasha's hands were doing before Sasha did, and who had spent eleven seconds that morning touching a bare wrist where a watch used to be, looked back at her and went white, and then red, and did not let go.</p>
